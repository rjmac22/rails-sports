# What Is a Game of Football?

## Why establish a football club?

Sporting clubs and societies had existed in Britain for generations, football among the games they organised. Some lasted for decades; others came and went. You know what I mean, the enthusiastic organiser gets busy with something else, people move away, somewhere to play becomes unavailable. The subscription goes up, travelling becomes expensive, somebody loses their job, or they simply decide the money would be better spent elsewhere. This was long before professional football leagues and careers. Keeping a club going depended on people having the time, money and enthusiasm to do it.

In October 1849, members of several cricket clubs in Surrey met to establish a football club. At the meeting, one speaker recalled that some sixty years earlier, a society formed by men from Westmorland and Cumberland had organised wrestling and football in London.

The society had played football on Kennington Common, including a substantial match between men representing the two counties in 1789. But when leading members retired and moved away, the society dissolved. Wrestling continued elsewhere, while football gradually declined in the area as suitable places to play became unavailable.

The Surrey organisers wanted to revive the activity. They established a club, collected subscriptions, provided equipment and arranged games on Wednesdays and Saturdays throughout the winter.

At Blackheath in the late 1850s, former pupils of Blackheath Proprietary School wanted to continue playing the football they had learned. Initially, they organised informal games among themselves, but regularly struggled to assemble enough former pupils to make up the teams.

They began recruiting outsiders, eventually opening participation more widely and establishing a formal club for the 1862–63 season.

Unlike Surrey, where the organisers wanted to revive football after earlier arrangements had disappeared, Blackheath's problem was finding enough available players. Expanding participation offered a solution.

## Why play against other clubs?

In Sheffield, Hallam FC was established in 1860 with connections to the existing Hallam Cricket Club.

According to Hallam's later history, some of its members wanted a less exclusive alternative to Sheffield FC. But John Charles Shaw, an early Sheffield member who helped establish Hallam, had another reason.

Writing in 1907, Shaw recalled how Sheffield had repeatedly rearranged its members into different teams to keep matches interesting. He helped establish Hallam, he explained, specifically to provide Sheffield with another club to play.

The two clubs met at Hallam's Sandygate ground on Boxing Day 1860. Sixteen players appeared on each side, rather than the eleven familiar in modern football. They played under the local Sheffield rules, without needing a national governing body to organise the contest.

Sheffield had already found occasional opponents among soldiers stationed nearby. But a separate club offered the possibility of regular competition against another established group. Shaw remembered the matches between Sheffield and Hallam as particularly enjoyable, although his account was written almost fifty years afterwards.

Other clubs appeared in the area. By 1862, eleven teams were active around Sheffield, playing under local rules. Each new club created more potential opponents for those already established, making outside competition increasingly accessible.

## Whose rules are we playing?

By 1862, eleven teams were playing football in the Sheffield area under broadly similar rules. But elsewhere, players had developed different ideas about how football should be played.

Schools throughout Britain had their own football traditions. Some had written rules, while others relied on established customs. At prestigious public schools such as Eton, Rugby, Harrow and Winchester, the differences could be substantial. What players were permitted to do with the ball, how they challenged opponents and how they scored depended on the game they had learned.

Those differences became apparent when former pupils met at university or joined football clubs.

At Cambridge, students from different schools had been attempting to agree on common playing rules since at least the 1840s. A surviving set from approximately 1856 bears the names of representatives associated with Eton, Rugby, Harrow and Shrewsbury.

The students had negotiated rules under which they could play together without requiring their former schools to change their own games.

But agreeing on a common game was not always straightforward.

In December 1858, a correspondent calling himself *Juvenis* wrote to *Bell's Life in London*, complaining about the difficulties caused by schools playing different versions of football. He argued that common rules would make matches between them easier to arrange.

The newspaper's editor suggested that smaller schools might adopt Eton's published rules.

A correspondent identifying himself with Rugby School immediately objected. Rugby had published its rules too. Why should Rugby footballers abandon their game in favour of Eton's? He suggested that representatives of the schools should instead meet and negotiate a compromise.

Other former pupils joined the argument. Winchester supporters defended their school's football, with one claiming its rules were older than those of the other schools. An Etonian maintained that his school's existing rules worked perfectly well for matches against university teams. Some questioned whether schools playing different games needed to compete against one another at all.

The correspondence continued into January 1859. The disagreements became increasingly personal, until the editor announced that further letters would not be published because they contained too much abuse of other contributors.

The newspaper had invited a discussion about common football rules. It ended up with former pupils arguing over whose version of football was best.

## What are you prepared to give up?

In October 1863, a correspondent calling himself *Etonensis* complained about football matches between former pupils of Eton and Westminster.

The two groups had been playing under compromise rules for two years. They had managed to agree on a game, but according to *Etonensis*, neither side could properly demonstrate the skills it had developed playing its own version of football.

The compromise had made the matches possible without necessarily making them satisfactory.

He wanted a common set of rules that footballers could learn and use against a wider range of opponents. But deciding what those rules should be remained a problem. What one group considered an essential part of football, another might regard as unnecessary or even dangerous.

On 26 October 1863, representatives of football clubs gathered at the Freemasons' Tavern in London to establish the Football Association.

They hoped to agree on rules that clubs could use when playing one another. But even among those willing to participate, there was disagreement about what sort of game they should play.

That disagreement became particularly apparent at a meeting in December.

Francis Maule Campbell, representing Blackheath, defended a version of football that allowed players to run with the ball and opponents to stop them by physical means, including *hacking*—kicking an opponent's shins.

Campbell regarded these challenges as part of what made football worth playing. The game tested courage and determination. Removing those features, he argued, would take away much of its character.

Ebenezer Cobb Morley of Barnes saw the problem differently.

Morley wasn't especially troubled by hacking himself, but he doubted that many adult footballers would willingly participate in a game that permitted it. Men with businesses and occupations to attend to had good reasons to avoid injuries that could prevent them from working.

The argument wasn't simply about whether hacking was dangerous. Campbell valued the physical contest it created. Morley believed retaining it would discourage people the association hoped to attract.

Neither was prepared to accept the other's preferred solution.

The meeting voted to remove the provisions permitting running with the ball and hacking. Blackheath subsequently withdrew from the association rather than accept a game that had lost features its members valued.

**The Football Association had succeeded in agreeing on rules. It had also lost a club in the process.**

## Why establish common rules?

While the association's members debated what their game should look like, clubs elsewhere were considering whether common rules—or membership of the association itself—would be useful.

In November 1863, John G. Tupholme, secretary of Louth Football Club in Lincolnshire, wrote to his counterpart at Lincoln FC. The clubs had already played each other, and Louth was considering revising its rules.

Tupholme favoured joining the new Football Association, but he made an important distinction. Whether the two clubs joined the London organisation or not, he wanted them to adopt common rules.

The reason was simply to make arranging their matches easier.

Tupholme wasn't proposing anything revolutionary. The clubs already knew how to organise football matches. They just wanted to remove one recurring inconvenience. And **they didn't need to join the Football Association to do it.**

The new association nevertheless offered something useful. Clubs could subscribe, help establish a common code and participate in future discussions about the rules. The agreed laws would be published, allowing other organisations to adopt them.

The association's secretary, Ebenezer Cobb Morley, believed that if twenty or thirty clubs agreed to play under the same rules, others would recognise the advantages of joining them.

But the association had no authority to compel outsiders to comply. Nor did its members necessarily agree with everything it proposed.

Sheffield FC, for example, welcomed the initiative and applied to join. Its secretary, William Chesterman, enclosed Sheffield's existing laws along with several objections to the association's proposals.

Sheffield wanted common rules. It also wanted a say in what those rules would be.

Other footballers were interested in the rules without wanting to join the organisation.

In Richmond, Edwin Ash helped arrange football among men who were temporarily living in the area, many preparing for military careers. He explained that there was little point establishing a permanent club when its members would soon be leaving.

Nevertheless, he expected them to adopt the association's laws. Using rules recognised by other clubs would make arranging matches more convenient.

On 8 December, the association finally approved its first playing code. Eleven days later, Richmond played Barnes under the new rules. The match finished 0–0.

The rules could therefore be useful even to footballers who had no intention of becoming association members.

But agreeing to try a common code didn't mean a club would necessarily like it.

Lincoln initially supported the association and experimented with its laws. Less than a year later, it withdrew after finding the experience unsatisfactory.

Louth took a different approach. In February 1864, its members agreed to adopt the FA's rules, but with exceptions to provisions concerning team numbers and handling the ball.

The neighbouring clubs had recognised the advantages of common rules. They had not reached identical conclusions about which rules they wanted to play under.

The Football Association had established a code that any willing club could use. But other versions of football remained popular, and existing clubs continued making their own arrangements.

Sheffield, for example, continued playing under its local rules. In 1865 it experimented with a stricter offside restriction, partly to accommodate competition against other clubs. The experiment proved unpopular, and Sheffield subsequently returned to a more permissive arrangement.

Even when footballers accepted the advantages of common rules, they could discover that the resulting game was less enjoyable than the one they had been playing.

The association's foundation was therefore not the moment when everybody finally agreed on football. It was the establishment of one more way for people to agree.

Its subsequent success would depend on something the founders could not settle in a committee meeting: **whether enough footballers actually wanted to play the game they had devised.**

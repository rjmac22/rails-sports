# What Is a Game of Football?

## Why establish a football club?

Sporting clubs and societies had existed in Britain for generations, football among the games they organised. Some lasted for decades; others came and went. You know what I mean, the enthusiastic organiser gets busy with something else, people move away, somewhere to play becomes unavailable. The subscription goes up, travelling becomes expensive, somebody loses their job, or they simply decide the money would be better spent elsewhere. This was long before professional football leagues and careers. Keeping a club going depended on people having the time, money and enthusiasm to do it.

In October 1849, members of several Surrey cricket clubs gathered at the Oval to establish a football club. During the meeting, the chairman recalled an earlier sporting organisation that had disappeared.

Some sixty years before, a group of men originally from Westmorland and Cumberland had established the Gymnastic Society in London. They organised wrestling and football, playing the latter on Kennington Common. In 1789, twenty-two men from Westmorland faced twenty-two from Cumberland. Westmorland won.

But when several leading members retired from business and moved away, the Gymnastic Society dissolved. Wrestling continued elsewhere, but football became increasingly difficult to organise as suitable places to play became unavailable.

The Surrey organisers wanted to revive the game. They established a new football club, agreed rules, collected subscriptions to pay for equipment and scheduled games on Wednesdays and Saturdays throughout the winter.

In the late 1850s, former pupils of Blackheath Proprietary School wanted to continue playing the football they played at school. Initially, they organised informal games among themselves, but regularly struggled to get enough former pupils to make up the teams.

They began inviting outsiders to play and eventually established a formal club for the 1862–63 season.

## Why play against other clubs?

Sheffield FC was founded in 1857. For the next three years, its members mostly played among themselves, dividing into different teams to keep the matches interesting. They occasionally played against soldiers stationed nearby, but had no other local football club to play regularly.

In 1860, one of its members, John Charles Shaw, decided to do something about that. He helped establish Hallam FC, giving Sheffield another club to play against.

The two clubs met at Hallam's Sandygate ground on Boxing Day 1860. The match had been advertised as sixteen-a-side, but severe weather kept several players away. Sheffield played with fewer men than Hallam and still won 2–0. They played under the local Sheffield rules.

Writing in 1907, nearly fifty years after the match, Shaw remembered the games between Sheffield and Hallam as particularly enjoyable.

Other clubs appeared in the area. By 1862, eleven teams were active around Sheffield, playing under local rules. It was a network effect: a new club had potential opponents to play, while the existing clubs gained another. The more clubs there were, the more attractive it became to start one.

## Whose rules are we playing?

The clubs around Sheffield played under broadly similar rules. But elsewhere, footballers had very different ideas about how the game should be played.

Across Britain, football was played under different rules. What players could do with the ball, how they challenged opponents and how they scored depended on the game they had learned.

At Cambridge, students who had learned different versions of football tried to agree on a set of rules they could all play under. They had been attempting this since at least the 1840s, and a set of rules from around 1856 still survives.

But agreeing on a common game was not always straightforward.

In December 1858, a reader calling himself *Juvenis* wrote to *Bell's Life in London*. He argued that it would be much easier for schools to arrange matches against one another if they could agree on the rules.

The newspaper's editor suggested that smaller schools might adopt Eton's published rules.

A Rugby supporter immediately objected. Rugby had published its own rules, so why should its footballers abandon their game in favour of Eton's? He suggested that representatives from the schools should meet and negotiate a compromise.

Others joined the argument. A Winchester supporter claimed their rules were older than those of the other schools. An Etonian insisted his school's rules worked perfectly well for matches against university teams. Some even questioned why schools playing different games needed to compete against one another at all.

The argument continued into January 1859, until the editor refused to publish any more letters because the contributors had started insulting one another.

## What are you prepared to give up?

In October 1863, a reader calling himself *Etonensis* complained that Eton and Westminster had been playing matches under compromise rules for two years, but neither side could properly demonstrate its skills. Moves that worked under one school's rules were forbidden under the other's. The matches could go ahead, but according to *Etonensis*, the standard of football was poor.

He wanted a common set of rules that footballers could learn and use against different opponents. But what should those rules be? What one group considered an essential part of football, another might regard as unnecessary or even dangerous.

On 26 October 1863, representatives of football clubs gathered at the Freemasons' Tavern in London to establish the Football Association.

At a meeting in December, Francis Maule Campbell, representing Blackheath, defended a version of football that allowed players to run with the ball and opponents to stop them by physical means, including *hacking*—kicking an opponent's shins.

Campbell regarded these challenges as part of what made football worth playing. The game tested courage and determination. Removing those features, he argued, would take away much of its character.

Ebenezer Cobb Morley of Barnes saw things differently. He wasn't particularly bothered by hacking himself, but doubted how many adults would want to play a game that allowed it. A broken leg could keep you off work for weeks, and turning up to meet customers looking like you'd had the crap kicked out of you wasn't exactly good for business. Not much has changed there.

Campbell wasn't going to let it pass quietly. He challenged the way an earlier vote had been counted, accused the other side of manoeuvring against the hacking supporters, and tried to postpone the decision until more school representatives could attend. That motion lost 13–4. Then the meeting voted 11–5 to strike out the rules allowing players to run with the ball and opponents to hack them.

A week later, on 8 December, Campbell announced that Blackheath was leaving the Football Association. But he hadn't fallen out with the whole enterprise: he agreed to stay on as its treasurer until the next general meeting.

**The Football Association had agreed on its rules. By January 1864, six of its eleven founding clubs had gone.**

Blackheath hadn't stopped playing football. It just didn't want to play the FA's version. What happened to its game is another story.

## Why establish common rules?

In November 1863, John G. Tupholme, secretary of Louth Football Club in Lincolnshire, wrote to his counterpart at Lincoln FC. The clubs had already played each other, and Louth was considering revising its rules.

Tupholme liked the idea of joining the new Football Association. But even if Louth and Lincoln decided not to join, he still wanted them to play by the same rules.

Morley had even pointed out, when arguing for common football rules, that cricket had been doing this for years. The MCC published the laws, and other clubs played by them without having to join.

So what was the point of joining the Football Association?

The new association nevertheless offered something useful. Clubs could subscribe, help establish a common code and participate in future discussions about the rules. The agreed laws would be published, allowing other organisations to adopt them.

The association's secretary, Ebenezer Cobb Morley, believed that if twenty or thirty clubs agreed to play under the same rules, others would recognise the advantages of joining them.

But the association had no authority to compel outsiders to comply. Nor did its members necessarily agree with everything it proposed.

Sheffield FC, for example, welcomed the initiative and applied to join. Its secretary, William Chesterman, enclosed Sheffield's existing laws along with several objections to the association's proposals.

Sheffield wanted common rules. It also wanted a say in what those rules would be.

Other footballers were interested in the rules without wanting to join the organisation.

In Richmond, Edwin Ash helped arrange football among men who were temporarily living in the area, many preparing for military careers. He explained that there was little point establishing a permanent club when its members would soon be leaving.

Nevertheless, he expected them to adopt the association's laws. Using rules recognised by other clubs would make arranging matches more convenient.

On 8 December, the association finally approved its first playing code. Eleven days later, Richmond played Barnes under the new rules. The match finished 0–0.

The rules could therefore be useful even to footballers who had no intention of becoming association members.

But agreeing to try a common code didn't mean a club would necessarily like it.

Lincoln initially supported the association and experimented with its laws. Less than a year later, it withdrew after finding the experience unsatisfactory.

Louth took a different approach. In February 1864, its members agreed to adopt the FA's rules, but with exceptions to provisions concerning team numbers and handling the ball.

The neighbouring clubs had recognised the advantages of common rules. They had not reached identical conclusions about which rules they wanted to play under.

The Football Association had established a code that any willing club could use. But other versions of football remained popular, and existing clubs continued making their own arrangements.

Sheffield, for example, continued playing under its local rules. In September 1865, it agreed to try a stricter offside rule, while allowing games against local clubs to continue under the old rules if those clubs did not adopt the change. Sheffield also proposed a rules compromise with Nottingham. The stricter offside experiment proved unpopular and was eventually abandoned.

Agreeing to a rule did not guarantee that players would be satisfied with the resulting game.

The association's foundation was therefore not the moment when everybody finally agreed on football. It was the establishment of one more way for people to agree.

Its subsequent success would depend on something the founders could not settle in a committee meeting: **whether enough footballers actually wanted to play the game they had devised.**

# Reporting Question — Who Controls the Next Delivery?

This note follows a reader observation from the Headingley 2019 reporting: cricket can become a contest over **who gets to face the next delivery**, not simply how many runs are scored from the present one.

This is a reporting note, not article prose and not an attempt to decide the optimal tactics Australia should have used.

## The question

**How much control does each side actually have over who faces the next delivery?**

The answer from Headingley is: neither side controls it outright. Both sides can influence the state that the next delivery begins from.

## The structural mechanism

An over contains six valid balls, and overs are bowled alternately from the two ends of the pitch. This creates a repeated six-ball structure within which the batting pair can manage the strike.

For ordinary completed runs:

- an odd number of runs changes which batter is at which end;
- an even number leaves the same batter at the striker's end for the next delivery;
- because the next over is bowled from the opposite end, a single from the final ball of an over allows the same batter to face the first ball of the next over.

This is why the timing of a single can matter as much as the fact that it scores one run.

Primary rule source: MCC Law 17 — The Over.

## What England could control

Stokes and Leach could not decide the result of a delivery, but they could make choices that strongly influenced who faced the next one.

### Stokes could refuse a run

If Stokes hit the ball into space early in an over, he did not have to take an available single. At Headingley he repeatedly declined singles because taking one too early would expose Leach to several deliveries.

### Stokes could seek even numbers

Two runs reduced the target while returning Stokes to the same end for the next delivery. Boundaries likewise allowed him to keep facing.

### Stokes could seek a late single

A single late in the over could be valuable because it reduced the target while limiting Leach's exposure. A single from the final ball could allow Stokes to start the next over as striker.

### The pair had an explicit plan

Leach described the arrangement after the match: Stokes would face roughly four or five balls of an over and Leach one or two. Leach also said Stokes told him to back up and look for twos.

Stokes later described the same division of labour even more starkly: he would try to take almost all the deliveries and Leach's job was to survive the small number he had to face.

This is direct evidence that England were managing **future deliveries**, not merely accumulating runs one at a time.

## What Australia could control

Australia could not force Stokes to accept a single. Their control was indirect: they could change the environment in which he made that choice.

### Field placement changed the available scoring options

Cricket Australia's reconstruction reports that Paine eventually placed all but one fielder on the boundary when Stokes was facing.

That was intended to make boundaries harder and stop Stokes ending the chase quickly. But a deep field also made some singles and twos easier to obtain. That could help Stokes manage the strike.

Paine later recognised part of this problem. He said that when a field spreads, bowlers can become defensive in mindset even though he still wanted them thinking about taking the wicket.

### Bowler choice changed Stokes's options

Paine's return to Nathan Lyon late in the chase mattered because Stokes found Lyon harder to attack while maintaining his strike-management plan.

Stokes explicitly called Lyon's return good captaincy. He said he had been trying to attack the first half of each over after Leach arrived, but Lyon made him uncertain whether to continue that method or attempt to finish through ones and twos.

So Australia could influence who faced future balls not only with field position but by choosing a bowler who altered the scoring choices available to Stokes.

Source: Cricket Australia, Andrew Ramsey, *Inside the crazy final hour at Headingley*, 26 August 2019.

## The near run-out shows the limit of control

With two runs needed, Leach set off for a single while Stokes did not intend to run. Leach later explained that he was thinking about taking a late-over single so Stokes would have the strike afterwards; Stokes was thinking about finishing the match in that over.

Lyon fumbled the return and Leach survived.

This moment is useful because it prevents the story becoming too tidy. Even when both batters understood the broad plan, they did not perfectly coordinate every delivery.

The next state was not controlled. It was negotiated through decisions, execution, communication and chance.

Source: Sky Sports interview with Jack Leach, 3 September 2019; Cricket Australia final-hour reconstruction.

## What this tells us for Study 21

Headingley gives us a concrete example of the study's state-based view of cricket.

The sequence is not simply:

`delivery → runs → delivery → runs`

It is closer to:

`current match state → delivery → outcome → new match state → next delivery`

The new state includes more than the score. It includes **which batter is on strike** and **which bowler is operating**.

Both teams therefore make decisions in the present partly to shape the conditions of future deliveries.

This is relevant to the current article because it helps explain cricket as a game in which runs are accumulated while finite resources — wickets, deliveries and playing time — are consumed.

## Editorial boundary

For the current article, the useful point is the struggle to shape the next state.

Questions such as exactly where Paine should have placed individual fielders, whether a different field maximised Australia's win probability, or what the optimal bowling plan was belong to later tactics/field-setting work.

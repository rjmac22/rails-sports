## Idea: who faces the ball can matter as much as the run itself

### Trigger

While reading the Headingley 2019 reporting file, the phrase below needed explaining:

> England deliberately managed who faced which deliveries, while Australia tried to prevent boundaries without losing access to Leach.

The useful point is that a general reader may not immediately understand what that means. Once explained, it makes the final-wicket partnership much easier to understand.

### The basic mechanism: strike

Only one batter faces each delivery.

If the batters score an odd number of runs, usually one, they swap ends. The other batter therefore normally faces the next delivery.

If they score an even number — two, four or six — they finish back at the same ends and the same batter remains on strike for the next delivery within the over.

The end of an over changes which end the bowling comes from. Because the batters do not change ends simply because an over ends, a single from the final ball can have a different tactical consequence from a single early in the over.

### Why Stokes wanted to control the strike

When Jack Leach arrived, England needed 73 runs and Australia needed one wicket.

Ben Stokes was an elite batter who was already well set. Leach was England's number 11 and a much weaker batter.

England therefore did not want the remaining deliveries to be divided evenly between them. They wanted Stokes to face as many as possible.

Suppose Stokes begins an over on strike.

If he takes a single from ball one:

- Stokes scores one run.
- Stokes and Leach change ends.
- Leach now faces ball two.
- Australia may get five deliveries in that over at the weaker batter.

That can be a poor trade for England even though they have added a run.

Instead, Stokes could refuse an available single early in an over and try to score an even number:

- two runs: Stokes remains on strike;
- four runs: Stokes remains on strike;
- six runs: Stokes remains on strike.

Near the end of the over, the value of a single changes.

If Stokes takes one run from the sixth ball:

- he and Leach swap ends because of the run;
- the over then ends;
- the next over is bowled from the opposite end;
- Stokes can therefore face the first ball of the next over again.

This is the basic logic of "farming the strike" or "shielding the tailender".

### Why Australia wanted "access to Leach"

"Access to Leach" does not mean physically getting close to him. It means creating opportunities to bowl deliveries to him.

Australia did not need Stokes specifically as the final wicket. Either Stokes or Leach being dismissed would end England's innings and win Australia the match.

But the probability of dismissal was not the same for the two batters.

Stokes was set and batting extraordinarily well. Leach was the number 11 and much more vulnerable.

Australia's ideal tactical outcome could therefore be something like:

- Stokes takes a single early in an over;
- Leach comes onto strike;
- Australia gets several deliveries at the weaker batter;
- one wicket ends the match.

England wanted almost the exact opposite:

- Stokes faces most of the over;
- he scores the bulk of the runs;
- he avoids giving Leach unnecessary deliveries;
- he takes a late single when useful so that he can regain or preserve the strike for the next over.

### Why this creates a field-setting problem for Australia

Australia also needed to stop Stokes scoring boundaries because England still needed 73 runs when Leach arrived.

One obvious response is to move more fielders towards the boundary.

But that can create another problem.

A spread field may make it harder for Stokes to hit fours and sixes, but it can also leave easy singles available inside the field.

A single is not automatically good for Australia or good for England. Its value depends on when it is taken and who will face the next delivery.

For example:

- a Stokes single from ball one can be attractive to Australia because it exposes Leach for most of the over;
- a Stokes single from ball six can be attractive to England because it adds a run while allowing Stokes to face the next over as well.

So Australia's problem was not simply:

> Stop Stokes scoring runs.

It was closer to:

> Stop Stokes scoring enough runs to win, while still creating deliveries at Leach, the more vulnerable batter.

Those objectives can conflict.

### Connection to Study 21

This is a particularly useful real-world example of the Study 21 idea that the value of an event depends on match state.

Two singles are both worth exactly one run on the scoreboard, but they can have very different tactical values because they produce different next states.

The distinction can be expressed as:

`same immediate score change -> different next batter -> different future probabilities`

That is more useful than saying simply that Stokes "attacked" or "farmed the strike". It explains why he did it and why Australia's field placement mattered.

It also connects directly to the wider chain from Study 21:

`match state -> changing value of runs/wickets/deliveries -> tactical trade-offs -> field placement`

### Potential explanatory job in the article

This could be one of the strongest teaching moments for a general reader.

A reader who does not know much cricket can understand that one run always adds one to the score. The surprising part is discovering that two identical one-run outcomes can change the future of the match differently.

That lets the article move from a simple scoreboard view of cricket into the analytical idea that tactics operate by changing the probabilities attached to the next state.

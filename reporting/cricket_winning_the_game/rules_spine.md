# Cricket from First Principles — Formal Rules Spine

## Purpose

This is the minimum formal structure needed by the journalism piece. It is not a rules explainer. Its job is to establish why runs, wickets, deliveries and playing time can constrain a cricket match differently before the article moves into Headingley, Sydney and Kolkata.

## 1. Runs are the scoring unit

MCC Law 18.1 states that the score is reckoned in runs.

That gives us the basic quantity, but it does **not** by itself describe the state of a match. A score only becomes meaningful once we also know the innings, wickets, target and whatever limits how long the innings or match can continue.

Source:

- MCC — Law 18, Scoring Runs: https://www.lords.org/mcc/the-laws/scoring-runs

## 2. Winning depends on how the innings finish

MCC Law 16 distinguishes one-innings and two-innings matches. In both cases the winning side must finish with the greater relevant total. Law 16 also provides for ties and draws where the conditions for a win have not been reached.

For this article the important point is not the legal wording. It is that **the final score cannot be separated from the structure of the match**.

A Test can therefore end as a draw with one side still short of a fourth-innings target if the available playing opportunity expires before either side completes the conditions for victory. That is the formal basis underneath the Sydney example.

Source:

- MCC — Law 16, The Result: https://www.lords.org/mcc/the-laws/the-result

## 3. An innings can be constrained by wickets, overs or time

MCC Law 13 says a match can have one or two innings per side and allows innings to be limited by a prescribed number of overs or by time. It also lists ways an innings can be completed, including being all out or reaching the prescribed over/time limit.

This is the key structural bridge for Study 21:

**runs are accumulated while other resources are being consumed.**

Depending on format and state, the relevant constraints can include:

- wickets;
- legal deliveries / overs;
- playing time.

Source:

- MCC — Law 13, Innings: https://www.lords.org/mcc/the-laws/innings

## 4. Deliveries are discrete and overs contain six valid balls

MCC Law 17 states that an over consists of six valid balls. No-balls, wides and certain dead-ball events do not count among those six valid balls.

That distinction matters for the limited-overs example. When we say West Indies needed 19 from six in Kolkata, we mean six **valid** deliveries remained. A legal delivery used without enough scoring permanently consumes one of those opportunities; a no-ball or wide can change both runs and the number of legal balls still available.

Source:

- MCC — Law 17, The Over: https://www.lords.org/mcc/the-laws/the-over

## 5. The three international formats impose different constraints

ICC's format guide gives the simple structural comparison we need:

| Format | Innings per side | Scheduled delivery limit | Match-time structure | What can run out? |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Test | 2 | No fixed innings over limit | Up to five days | wickets and playing time |
| ODI | 1 | 50 overs | one-day match | wickets and legal deliveries |
| T20I | 1 | 20 overs | short-form match | wickets and legal deliveries |

This table deliberately describes the ordinary uninterrupted forms. Weather, revised targets and competition-specific playing conditions can alter the detailed mechanics.

ICC currently lists separate men's Test, ODI and T20I playing conditions. Its format guide describes Tests as five-day, two-innings matches, ODIs as one-innings matches of 50 overs per side, and T20Is as 20 overs per side.

Sources:

- ICC — The Three Formats of Cricket: https://www.icc-cricket.com/about/cricket/game-formats/the-three-formats
- ICC — Playing Conditions: https://www.icc-cricket.com/about/cricket/rules-and-regulations/playing-conditions

## The article-level version

The article should probably compress all of the above to something close to this conceptual sequence rather than reproducing the laws:

```text
Cricket counts runs.

But runs are scored inside constraints.

Test: wickets + playing time
ODI:  wickets + 50 overs
T20:  wickets + 20 overs

Those constraints change what the next delivery is worth.
```

Then the three matches show what that means in practice:

- **Headingley 2019:** the remaining deliveries are not equivalent because both sides care who faces them.
- **Sydney 2021:** surviving a delivery can itself be progress because it consumes Australia's remaining playing opportunity.
- **Kolkata 2016:** every legal delivery is part of a tiny fixed stock, so extracting six runs early can transform the remaining problem.

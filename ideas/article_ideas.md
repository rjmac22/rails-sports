An editorial holding area, not a publication schedule. Keep research proportionate to the story; prioritise getting the next article published.

## When professionals don't know their own sport's rules

**Status:** Candidate for a short, relatively straightforward article; not yet selected as Article 2.

**Core question:** How can someone become an expert at playing a sport without becoming an expert in its rules?

**Potential argument:** Playing skill, habitual rule knowledge, and knowledge of the complete rulebook are different kinds of expertise. Unfamiliar situations, rarely encountered provisions, and rule changes can expose gaps even among experienced professionals. We should investigate rather than assume ignorance is surprising or consequential.

**Possible starting incidents (verify original accounts, the precise rule, and outcomes before drafting):**

- **Donovan McNabb — 2008 Eagles–Bengals NFL tie.** The veteran quarterback said he did not know a regular-season game could end in a tie. Strong candidate for examining whether a misunderstood outcome could affect tactical choices. Do not assume his misunderstanding changed a decision without evidence.
- **Denis Shapovalov — 2022 Italian Open.** Crossed the net to dispute a ball mark; a code violation contributed to a game penalty. Said afterwards that he did not know the relevant rule. Good visual example with an identifiable decision and consequence.
- **Rory McIlroy — 2024 Pebble Beach Pro-Am.** Penalised two strokes for applying an older unplayable-ball relief procedure. Useful contrast: a rule *change*, not necessarily ignorance of a longstanding rule.
- **John McEnroe — 1990 Australian Open.** Disqualified after a sequence of code violations, reportedly unaware that the penalty sequence had changed. Another useful rule-change contrast; check contemporary reporting.

**Suggested scope:** Choose **one central incident** and, if genuinely useful, one comparison. Explain exactly what the player thought, what the rule actually said, why the misunderstanding could survive years of professional play, and what the consequence was. End with a defensible insight rather than a compilation of famous blunders.

**Research questions:**

1. What do official rules and contemporary reports establish? Did the player themselves acknowledge not knowing the rule?
2. How often would a player realistically encounter the rule in competition?
3. Did the rule change, or was it simply an unusual provision?
4. Did the misunderstanding demonstrably change an action, the score, or strategy?
5. Do referees, coaches, and players need different depths of rules expertise?

**Editorial guardrails:** Avoid a listicle, moralising about 'stupid players', or retelling a clip without adding understanding. Prefer official rulebooks, on-record player comments and footage. Distinguish proof of ignorance from journalists' interpretations. Aim for roughly 1,000–1,200 words only if the evidence merits them. No new data pipeline or large-scale modelling for this one.

**Related idea to keep separate:** Did football's 1992 back-pass rule actually reduce time-wasting or speed up play? That would require reliable before/after measures, attention to confounders, and a clear definition of 'sped up'. Do not claim success from intent alone.

## Rule Change Investigations — topical editorial format

**Status:** Recurring article format / opportunity to watch when a sport announces a rule change. Not necessarily the next article, and not a commitment to cover every announcement.

**Core premise:** A rule change responds to a *perceived* problem. The story is not simply what the rule says or what problem administrators claim to be solving, but whether the problem was correctly diagnosed, the proposal makes sense, and the consequences match its intentions.

### Phase 1 — At announcement: examine the decision before the outcome is known

Possible article question: **Has the governing body identified the right problem, and is this rule likely to solve it?**

1. **Problem definition:** What is supposedly wrong? Is there measurable evidence, or mainly complaints, publicity, or isolated incidents?
2. **Whose objective?** Who benefits — players, teams, referees, spectators, broadcasters, sponsors or administrators? Could their interests conflict?
3. **Decision process:** Who proposed the change, what alternatives were discussed, and what objections or trade-offs were recorded?
4. **Mechanism and incentives:** By what causal pathway is the new rule meant to help? How could rational competitors change their behaviour in response?
5. **Predictions:** What intended improvements, side effects and unexpected adaptations are plausible? Distinguish what evidence supports from speculation.
6. **Predeclared test:** Before implementation, specify the outcome measures, baselines and time window that would demonstrate success, failure or an inconclusive result.

**Editorial value:** Timely, original analysis alongside the routine announcement coverage and reactions. Even a short calculation, historical comparison or incentive diagram can contribute; no elaborate data pipeline unless justified.

### Phase 2 — After implementation: test the predictions

If the rule is sufficiently important and data permit a follow-up:

- Compare outcomes with the *predictions recorded in Phase 1* — including those we got wrong.
- Measure whether the identified problem actually improved, using clear definitions and comparable before/after evidence where possible.
- Look for changes in tactics, workarounds, displacement effects, costs to other stakeholders and genuinely unintended consequences (positive as well as negative).
- Consider other changes occurring at the same time and the counterfactual: what might have happened without the rule?
- Distinguish *the rule achieved its narrow aim* from *the rule was good for the sport overall*. A governing body may have optimised the wrong objective.

### Selection and workflow

- Watch **newly announced** changes across major and less-covered sports; do not confine the idea to football or F1.
- Choose an announcement with an identifiable decision, accessible rule text, recorded reasoning/debate, a plausible mechanism and something observable to test. An apparently boring rule with interesting incentives may be better than a headline-grabbing one.
- Source the primary announcement, governing-body documents, consultations/meeting records if public, official rulebook, contemporaneous objections and relevant baseline evidence.
- Publish promptly while the decision is topical. Do not let the project become an endless survey of sports regulation.
- Preserve dated, falsifiable predictions in research notes/notebook so a later article cannot quietly rewrite them.
- **Possible outputs:** a timely pre-implementation investigation; a follow-up evaluation only where worthwhile; occasionally a retrospective when evidence is already available.

**Possible examples to investigate, not pre-selected conclusions:** 2025 Monaco GP compulsory two stops and tactical backing-up; NFL dynamic kickoff revisions and touchback incentives; football's goalkeeper eight-second holding rule; rugby union's 20-minute red-card trial. Verify dates, official reasoning and outcomes individually if selected. The historical 1992 football back-pass rule is a separate data-dependent retrospective idea (was time-wasting reduced, and did play speed up?).

**Guardrails:** Do not presume every rule change is solving a real problem, that unintended effects are automatically bad, or that an observed after-change movement was caused by the rule. Report uncertainty and conflicting stakeholders fairly. **A good discovery may be that the change worked, failed, had little effect, or addressed the wrong question entirely.**

## Bad sports commentary — does it help viewers understand the game?

**Status:** Candidate article idea, not yet selected. Origin: viewer's frustration with misleading claims and MotoGP commentators talking very fast, loudly and over each other.

**Core question:** What does commentary add for someone who can already see the action? When does it inform, mislead or overwhelm the viewer?

**Two separate problems to investigate:**

1. **Analytical/factual quality:** hindsight treated as foresight; outcome bias (successful tactic = brilliant, unsuccessful = stupid); speculative mind-reading of players; misleading statistics without denominators or context; stock clichés presented as explanations; confident but incorrect rules or strategy claims; simply narrating visible action without adding information.
2. **Delivery and attention:** speech rate, excessive volume or intensity, constant talking, overlapping commentators, interruptions, and whether the broadcast leaves space for pictures and natural sound. MotoGP commentary was raised as a possible case to *test*, not an established finding.

**Possible manageable study:** Select one short, accessible segment of a race or match. Transcribe commentary with timestamps; watch the footage; annotate (a) information visible without commentary, (b) genuinely new and useful context, (c) testable factual assertions, (d) interpretation/speculation, (e) errors or unsupported assertions. For the delivery question, record speaking time, approximate words per minute, and overlapping speech. Optionally compare viewing with the original audio and with commentary muted, recognising that an individual reaction is not evidence of average audience effects.

**Interesting comparisons:** TV versus radio (radio *must* describe what a listener cannot see); expert colour analysis versus play-by-play; live uncertainty versus hindsight; quiet versus high-energy sports and exciting moments versus routine phases.

**Potential angle:** *Commentary that sounds knowledgeable is not necessarily commentary that makes a viewer more knowledgeable.* Avoid a mere blooper compilation or a personal attack on commentators. Distinguish factual errors from subjective taste, excitement or legitimately different audience needs; do not assume that faster, louder commentary is bad for everyone.

**Research guardrails:** Make any criticism specific, check full context and official rules, quote brief excerpts accurately, and take care with audiovisual rights. Start with a contained clip and a clear question; avoid building a vast cross-sport commentary database.

## Retrospective rule-change case study — ODI two-ball rule (parked)

**Reviewed:** 19 October 2026. **Decision: NO DRAFT.** Research was promising but we could not establish a distinct enough story to pitch after existing coverage. Keep as a **research lead**, not an active writing assignment. Do not resurrect it merely because new statistics appear.

**Origin:** BBC Sport's 19 October 2026 piece on the return of ODI reverse swing prompted an editorial exercise: can we tell the history of the 2011 two-new-balls decision, its objections, the 2025 partial reversal, and players' adaptations *as a story* rather than as a collection of before/after averages?

**Historical timeline and promising scenes (source-check before publication):**
- **2007:** White-ball replacement after 34 overs; Brett Lee objected that receiving a newer replacement undermined the possibility of reverse swing. Earlier roots mean **"the rule took 14 years to reverse" is an oversimplification**.
- **October 2011:** ICC introduces two new balls, one from each end; ICC cricket manager David Richardson acknowledges concerns about reverse swing but expects skilful bowlers can still achieve it within 25 overs' use. The decision was intended partly to address visibility and balance between bat and ball.
- **2012:** ICC committee describes the two-ball change as successful at that stage. Avoid rewriting this as if its failure had always been accepted.
- **June 2018:** England make 481–6 against Australia; Sachin Tendulkar and Waqar Younis criticise the two-ball system. **The innings sparked debate; it does not prove that the ball rule caused 481.**
- **2019/2023:** Karthik S and Jarrod Kimber publish differing statistical analyses of the pre/post-2011 effects. Important counterevidence: there may have been *benefits* to pace bowlers with the new ball and some spinners, as well as a loss of late reverse swing.
- **July 2025:** New ODI condition: after 34 overs using two balls, fielding team chooses one for the remaining 16; chosen ball may accumulate up to 33 overs' use (vs 25 previously). A similar 30-over compromise had been publicly discussed earlier; don't claim causal influence on ICC without documentation.
- **2025–26:** Players/coaches discuss attacking earlier and preparing the older ball. Candidate scenes to verify fully: Taskin Ahmed's successive reverse-swing dismissals of Xavier Bartlett and Adam Zampa (Australia v Bangladesh, June 2026); England's acceleration ahead of over 35 against Sri Lanka at The Oval (September 2026). **Neither scene by itself shows that the rule decided the match or caused a strategic shift.**
- **October 2026:** BBC/CricViz reports more late-innings pace swing, but changes in death-over economy are modest and vary by team and conditions.

**Unresolved research question (possible future revival):** *Has the 2025 rule restored an important bowling skill without materially changing overall scoring or results?* The BBC's late-innings **pace-only** figures and broader **all-bowler** scoring analyses are not directly comparable; apparent disagreement may be sample composition, venue, innings phase or adaptation. Do not claim a contradiction until definitions and denominators align. A second question — *are teams genuinely attacking earlier?* — already has published coverage; only revisit for a strong new match, interview, or independently replicated finding.

**If revisited, minimum evidence needed:**
1. Download/validate Cricsheet ODI ball-by-ball records; compare closely matched periods before/after July 2025, with match counts, innings phase (especially overs 26–34 and 43–50), wickets in hand, opposition, venue, innings targets and bowlers. Cricsheet does **not** track swing itself.
2. Identify one consequential *decision* in an actual match, corroborated by on-record participants and ball-by-ball evidence. Separate correlation, players' own tactical reasoning, and proof of causal effect.
3. Recheck original ICC documents and source articles, plus competing statistical analyses; establish what the new piece adds beyond existing coverage.
4. **Reopen only if the answer is surprising, defensible and demonstrably not already told.** Otherwise leave parked.

**Editorial lessons:** Statistics without a consequence prompt "**So what?**"; do not glue an ordinary defeat onto a rules explainer and call it a story. Trace *intention → objection/prediction → actual decisions → consequences → competing explanations*, and check that an editor has a reason to commission it **now**. Historical rule-change retrospectives are a useful *story-finding method*, not an obligation to produce an article from every rule change. **Priority remains publishing the articles already underway.**

## Rory Delap and Stoke — how do you recognise an advantage *before* it works?

**Added:** 20 October 2026. **Status:** Idea only; investigate if it yields a genuinely original story, not an automatic next article.

**Origin:** Critique of *From Dugout to Boardroom*, “Everyone knew what Rory Delap was going to do. It still worked” (20 Oct 2026). [Original LinkedIn post](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/fromdugouttoboardroom-management-leadership-ugcPost-7518205005401595906-JYt3/). The post uses Stoke's 2–1 win over Arsenal on 1 November 2008 to illustrate the resource-based view of competitive advantage. It claims seven of Stoke's nine 2008–09 throw-in goals came by early November; verify definitions and underlying sources before repeating as fact.

**Better central question:** *Rory Delap had possessed his extraordinary long throw for years. Why did Stoke turn it into a weapon, and how could anyone have recognised that opportunity before the goals arrived?*

**Editorial approach:** The football is the subject. Start from a real puzzle and reconstruct decisions, tests, costs, alternatives, adaptations and consequences. **Do not begin with a predetermined management theory or announce a “lesson for business”.** Let readers draw their own connections about overlooked abilities and making choices under uncertainty. An optional management insight should be earned through evidence rather than bolted on. Recognition is not discovery; describing a successful outcome isn't explaining how to achieve it.

**Questions to investigate:**
1. When did Delap first develop and use the long throw? How was it employed at previous clubs, and what did coaches say at the time?
2. What did Tony Pulis and Stoke actually *notice*, decide and change? Find contemporaneous interviews, footage and tactical accounts; avoid attributing a deliberate discovery process without evidence.
3. What were the actual opportunities and costs? Winning territory and suitable throw-ins, positioning and training teammates, recruitment, other attacking options sacrificed, risk of relying on one player.
4. What is the correct denominator? Goals from throw-ins per attempt, opportunities, matches, time and league baseline; other set pieces; comparable teams and seasons. Separate Delap's contribution from Stoke's wider survival performance.
5. How quickly and how exactly did opponents adapt? Did conversion decline, did frequency/opportunity change, or were early numbers just a small sample? Do not confuse a tactical edge with a durable strategic advantage.
6. Find a useful counterexample: another unusual player skill or tactical novelty that *wasn't* worth a team's investment, or a comparable ability used differently. This guards against selecting successes retrospectively.

**Possible practical reader takeaway (not preordained):** A way to recognise a promising capability: observe it, ask what problem it solves, devise a cheap test, compare it with alternatives using explicit measures, evaluate complementarity and opportunity costs, then decide whether to develop, abandon or adapt it. Only suggest this if the historical evidence supports it; distinguish a practical framework we devise from what Stoke demonstrably did.

**Editorial / commercial lesson from the source article:** A football anecdote illustrating the obvious (“use your strengths”) provides little explanation of *how* and no actionable method. Avoid the formula **management theory → famous football example → obvious conclusion**. Prefer **unanswered sporting question → research → defensible discovery**. Source material can be a prompt for an independent investigation, not something to copy or paraphrase as original.

**Go/no-go:** Before drafting, verify a genuinely surprising, documentable decision or discovery and evidence of its consequence. If none exists, park it. **Do not let this displace completing and publishing current work.**

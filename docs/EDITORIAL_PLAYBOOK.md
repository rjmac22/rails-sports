# Rails Sports Editorial Playbook

This is the working editorial and production process for Rails Sports. It should evolve from real article work rather than from invented rules.

## Roles

**Production/subediting:** ChatGPT handles routine production work: structural review, factual verification, consistency checks, house-style checks, source verification, presentation options, captions, charts, metadata, SEO and other publication mechanics.

**Editor/publisher:** The author supervises, decides editorial intent, approves meaningful changes, chooses between genuine alternatives and sets Rails Sports' voice and standards.

Routine checks do not need to be escalated when they pass. Problems, uncertainties and choices that affect meaning, voice or publication policy do.

## Copy status

Treat finished body copy as locked by default.

A prose change needs a specific editorial reason: factual error, ambiguity for the intended reader, structural or logical failure, legal/ethical issue, established house-style inconsistency, or a production problem that genuinely harms reading.

"I would phrase this differently" is not enough.

Presentation furniture remains provisional after body copy is locked: headline, standfirst, section headings/crossheads, images, captions, charts, pull quotes, source treatment, links, metadata and SEO.

## Subediting sequence

1. **Identify the article.** Define what kind of piece it is and its actual editorial purpose.
2. **Identify the reader.** State what knowledge can reasonably be assumed and what needs explaining.
3. **Identify the medium.** Rails Sports long-form pieces are currently web-first and must work cleanly on mobile as well as desktop.
4. **Check house-style precedent.** The first published Rails Sports piece is the baseline. Keep an existing convention when it works; change it only for a stated reason.
5. **Read once as a reader.** Read the whole piece at normal pace before editing.
6. **Check structure before sentences.** Ask whether the article has a clear narrative/argument thread, whether sections follow logically and whether transitions do their job.
7. **Check the intro as its own production step.** Confirm that the opening gives the intended reader a reason to continue, accurately frames the article's real promise and does not promise a different piece from the one that follows.
8. **Do not change copy merely to show that it has been edited.** A structural pass can legitimately end in PASS — no changes required.
9. **Run the production fact-check.** Verify names, dates, scores, quotations, figures, roles, attributions and other checkable assertions against the reporting record and reliable sources. Do not restart the research phase unless a check exposes a problem.
10. **Resolve consistency and house-style issues.** Add recurring decisions to `editorial/house-style.md`.
11. **Build the publication layer only after the copy is sound.** Then work on headline, standfirst, images, captions, charts, pull quotes, source presentation, links, metadata, SEO and layout.
12. **Final proof.** Check the complete published object, not just the prose: body copy, furniture, links, labels, charts, captions, credits, metadata and mobile presentation.

## Current article lessons

For *What does it mean to win a game of cricket?*:

- Article type: analytical feature.
- Editorial spine: rules -> constraints -> incentives -> decisions.
- Reader: sports-literate and basically cricket-literate, without assumed specialist tactical or analytical knowledge.
- Primary medium: web-first long-form, with mobile treated as a first-class constraint.
- Structural subedit: PASS; no prose changes required.
- Full production fact-check: completed; factual claims, quotations, match states and betting-market evidence were checked against the reporting record and source trail.

## Updating this playbook

Add a rule only when article work reveals a reusable editorial principle. Keep article-specific decisions in the article's reporting files unless they generalise to future Rails Sports work.


## Digital packaging

Treat the finished article as canonical copy and adapt the publication wrapper to the channel.

A web article may need a display headline, SEO/search title, standfirst or description, social-sharing text, links, images and metadata. These elements may differ when the medium gives them genuinely different jobs, but do not create variants merely for the sake of having variants.

Do not force the body copy to carry search or distribution functions that can be handled cleanly by metadata or presentation furniture. Preserve the article's voice while making its subject clear to search engines, feeds, social previews and mobile readers.


## Link policy

Links must have a specific reader or publication job: explanation, context, attribution, licence compliance or navigation to genuinely related material.

Do not hyperlink every factual claim. Keep the reading experience clean and place the detailed evidential trail in the research notebook or source notes unless the reader benefits from seeing a source immediately.

Use descriptive anchor text that tells the reader what the destination is. Avoid generic wording such as "click here" or "read more".


## Media asset policy

Images, video, audio and other embedded media must earn their place editorially and be legally usable.

Before using any media asset, check both:

1. **Editorial value:** does it add information, evidence, atmosphere, explanation or understanding rather than merely decorate the page?
2. **Rights and publication status:** is there a clear basis for publishing or embedding it, and are any attribution, licence or platform requirements satisfied?

Do not shortlist attractive assets for publication until their usability has been checked. Prefer media that helps the reader see or understand something the prose alone cannot show as efficiently.


## Tags and metadata

Use tags to classify content for retrieval, related-content systems and archive management, not as a second headline or a bag of vague keywords.

Choose tags that are accurate, specific and reusable across multiple pieces. Avoid near-duplicates, redundant variants and tags that merely repeat an obvious section/category label.

Keep a controlled vocabulary where practical. Add a new tag only when it creates a useful distinction for future discovery or grouping.


## Social promotion

Treat social copy as a compact version of the article's real editorial promise, not as a separate clickbait layer.

Social text should be concise, platform-appropriate and accurate. It should surface the strongest genuine question, tension, finding or consequence in the piece without overstating the evidence or promising something the article does not deliver.

Write social copy after the article and its main presentation are settled. Different platforms may need different wording, but the underlying promise must remain the same.


## Mechanical proof

Before publication, run a separate mechanical proof after substantive editing is finished. Check spelling, grammar, names, titles, place names, figures, calculations, attributions, punctuation and house-style consistency.

This is not another rewriting pass. Correct objective errors and inconsistencies; do not reopen settled prose merely because alternative phrasing is possible.


## Headline principle

A headline must do two jobs: tell the reader what the piece is about and give the right reader a reason to enter it.

For Rails Sports features, accuracy and clarity come before cleverness. Wordplay is optional and should never obscure the article's real subject or promise. The display headline and search/SEO title may differ when they have genuinely different jobs.

Do not overstate the article's evidence in the headline, standfirst, social copy or other presentation furniture. Packaging may sharpen the promise, but it must not make the claim stronger than the reporting supports.


## Headline and standfirst system

Treat the headline, standfirst and intro as a sequence, not three repetitions of the same sentence.

- The headline identifies the subject and creates a truthful reason to enter.
- The standfirst adds information, sharpens the angle and states the article's real promise.
- The intro begins doing the article's work rather than restating the furniture.

For analytical features, do not force a news-style active-verb headline where it distorts the piece. Curiosity is useful; confusion is not. A headline must still make sense when detached from the page in search, social or a feed.

Do not stop at the first serviceable headline. Generate alternatives, reject predictable first associations and check the connotations of important words. Cultural references, puns, rhyme, alliteration and other devices are optional; use them only when they add meaning rather than merely sounding clever.

A standfirst should normally be short. For Rails Sports analytical features, prefer an explainer-led standfirst with summary precision. An intriguing opening observation is useful only when it genuinely represents the whole argument.

## Captions and alt text

A caption should add information the reader cannot simply see for themselves. Prefer context, identification, analytical significance or useful technical detail over visual narration.

Never caption what the reader can already see when you can tell them why what they are seeing matters.

Check the caption against the actual crop. Do not claim that an image shows an exact moment, person, location or tactical detail unless that has been verified.

Alt text has a different job from a caption. It should describe the meaningful visual content needed by a reader using assistive technology. Do not mechanically duplicate the caption.

## Pull quotes, section headings and crossheads

Use pull quotes primarily to surface important ideas, not merely flashy sentences. Prefer exact wording. Alter wording only for a genuine production need and never in a way that changes meaning.

Use sectional subheads to mark major argumentative sections. Add internal crossheads only when a long section genuinely needs another doorway for the reader. Navigation comes before wit.

Do not introduce decorative or textless crossheads unless the design system gives them a clear purpose.

A long contextual crosshead or mini-summary is appropriate only when a reader needs orientation before an extract or self-contained section. Do not add one where the body already supplies the necessary context.

## Box-outs

Use box-outs for useful secondary material that would interrupt the main argument if inserted into the body.

Good candidates include compact rule explanations, score-state snapshots, methodology notes, supporting data, subsidiary narratives and other evidence that rewards interested readers without being essential to following the main line.

If information is necessary to understand the argument, it belongs in the article proper rather than being hidden in a box-out.

## Page livery and recurring production elements

Once recurring visual labels, section markers, credits, identifiers or other publication furniture have proved useful, make them systematic rather than reinventing them article by article.

Put recurring production checks into checklists. Do not rely on memory for routine publication mechanics.

## Metadata and discoverability

Metadata should describe and classify the actual subject of the article. Think topic and reader usefulness before keywords.

Use natural, accurate terms that readers are likely to use. Do not distort the editorial subject to chase a trend and do not stuff keywords.

Social metadata, hashtags and handles should improve discovery or attribution, not decorate a post. Tag people or organisations only when they are genuinely relevant.

## Legal and ethical production checks

The production editor is not the lawyer. The job is to recognise when a piece raises a legal question and escalate it rather than improvising legal certainty.

Editing can create legal or ethical problems even when the original copy is sound. Headlines, standfirsts, captions, crops, juxtaposition, punctuation and cuts can all strengthen or alter an implication.

Preserve the level of certainty, qualification, attribution and balance established by the reporting. Never sharpen a claim merely because it makes stronger furniture.

Distinguish fact from analysis and opinion. Critical conclusions should rest on evidence that can be shown.

Every external visual or multimedia asset requires a rights check before publication. Being publicly accessible online is not a rights status. Verify ownership, licence, attribution requirements and whether embedding or reuse is permitted.

Do not let an image, crop, caption or layout imply something the reporting does not support. Verify identities and context before publication.

If a future piece touches an active court case, formal allegation, serious reputational claim or other non-routine legal issue, stop the normal workflow and seek appropriate legal review.

Judge the ethical effect of the whole published package, not just whether each individual sentence is defensible.

## Comments and reader contributions

Do not assume reader comments, letters or social submissions are legally or editorially harmless merely because someone else wrote them. Publishing them creates moderation and verification responsibilities.

Rails Sports should not add on-site comments by default at launch. Reconsider only when there is a clear editorial benefit and a workable moderation policy.

## Hero visual and landing experience

The first screen should tell the right reader quickly what kind of piece this is and why it is worth their attention.

Use the headline, standfirst and hero visual as one coherent opening package. Prefer one strong visual idea over a collage that tries to represent every example in the article.

A hero image or graphic should communicate one thing clearly at a glance and should reinforce the article's real subject rather than merely show a famous person from it.

Keep the promise consistent across search/social wrapper, landing page and article. No bait-and-switch between discovery and reading.

When planning the landing experience, ask:
- what the intended reader already knows;
- what they are curious about;
- what this article gives them that ordinary coverage does not.

## Pre-publication distribution decision

Before self-publishing a finished feature, make an explicit pitch-first versus self-publish-first decision.

If the piece is plausibly commissionable elsewhere, use a short, bounded pitching window to a small number of suitable publications. Do not allow pitching to become an open-ended delay. If there is no prompt interest, publish and move on.

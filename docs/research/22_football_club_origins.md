# Study 22 — Football from First Principles
## Section 1: Why did people establish football clubs, and what did they actually do?

**Status:** Working research section (19 October 2026). Not an article, not a chronological history, and not a claim that one club's experience explains all football.

### Method

Ask in order:
1. **Who** wanted a club?
2. **What** did they want to achieve, and **why**?
3. What did they already have (members, workplace, church, field, money, another sport, established competitions)?
4. **What did the club actually do** — week to week and in its first seasons?
5. What problem, if any, did its arrangements solve? What costs and alternatives existed?
6. Which conclusions are supported by documents, which by later club recollection, and which remain hypotheses?

Do **not** begin by assuming eleven players, spectators, a league, standardised rules, winning trophies, or professionalism. Do not extrapolate the founding motives of one club to the whole sport.

The categories above are **not** one inevitable progression and are **not** representative proportions. Organisations entered football at different dates, with different objectives, under different existing conditions. A workplace or chapel affiliation describes *where participants came from*, not automatically *why every member participated*.

### What a club actually did: documented operating arrangements

**Edinburgh, 1824 onward:** An archive maintained by National Records of Scotland preserves subscriber lists from 1824–25 and 1831–32; a receipt for using Dalry Park to play football and practise archery (1826); and a receipt for blowing up footballs (1832). The club ran as a continuing membership organisation, not merely a one-off match.

**Sheffield, 1857–1859:** The surviving minute books and printed rules document:
- A recurring membership (about 62 by the end of the first season), subscriptions and audited accounts; £2 6s for six balls, plus beer and other expenses.
- An athletics day with entries, prizes and recorded winners, indicating club activities extended beyond football matches.
- Club governance: officers, a committee, annual meetings, election and admission rules.
- Subscriptions: printed club rules specify 2s 6d annually, with provisions for an additional call.
- Play time: Saturdays from 2pm until dark, season November–Easter Eve.
- Guest access and dispute resolution by committee members.
- Deliberation over the laws of the *game* in October 1858, distinct from the rules governing the *club*.
- In the early years, games among its own members; therefore an established club need not initially have external opponents or a league.

**Newton Heath, 1878:** Players were railway workers; the official Manchester United history says they played other departments of the railway and other railway companies. Existing workplace contacts helped define the playing group and the opposition. Precise early operational details (selection, player availability, payment, financing) are not established by that source.

**Dial Square/Royal Arsenal, 1886:** Arsenal's official history says that 15 men contributed sixpence each, Danskin added three shillings, and they bought a ball. For their first game they had no kit, no agreed name and no home ground. They nevertheless arranged a match against Eastern Wanderers, then subsequently chose a name and secured kit. In other words, not every club started by formalising all its arrangements.

### What follows — and what does not

**Supported:**
- Clubs could solve concrete organisation problems: recurring participation, collecting contributions, obtaining a place to play and equipment, agreeing rules, assigning governance.
- Those functions might sit within another organisation (a church, employer, cricket club) or be created independently.
- Clubs existed for different reasons, and some began when the surrounding sport was already commercial.
- Formal club rules and the playing laws were distinct things.

**Not yet supported:**
- That attendance difficulties were the dominant reason clubs formed.
- That ordinary members all wanted exactly the same outcome.
- That external fixtures were inherently more enjoyable than internal matches.
- That every club wanted to become professional, win leagues or attract paying spectators.
- That we can describe a representative 'typical early club' from one surviving archive.
- That the modern rules or league system were the necessary outcome of clubs' original problems.

### Still to establish before moving to leagues

- What share of members attended ordinary sessions? What happened when too few or too many arrived?
- How were games and teams organised and balanced? How did this differ by type of club?
- What were actual costs and time constraints: work, access to land, balls, clothes, transport?
- Who was empowered to select members and make decisions, and what did membership confer?
- At what point did a club decide to arrange matches with another club, and why was this worth the transaction costs?
- Did member organisations that *never* became professional or league members solve their original goals just as successfully?

## First comparative pass: What did clubs do, and why did anyone need one? (19 October 2026)

**Scope:** Early football groups in Edinburgh, Cambridge, Sheffield, Blackheath, Barnes, Hallam, Richmond and Melbourne, broadly 1824–1863. This pass is about participants, founding motives, coordination, costs, membership and operations **before** attempting to explain associations, leagues or professionalism. Several used games distinct from modern association football. Do not conflate their codes or assume any single teleology.

**Direct research finding:** It was possible to organise football with no formal club, and possible to create a club without controlling every match members played. The useful distinction is between the **activity** (people play), a **team** (a group representing itself for a match), and a **membership institution** (ongoing administration, resources, decision rights, subscriptions and potentially a continuing identity). Actual people might slide between these rather than consciously choosing a legal structure.

### Case A — John Hope's Edinburgh Foot-Ball Club (1824–1841)

**Who/why:** Student lawyer John Hope and members, many affluent young men connected via educational and professional networks, organised sustained football recreation. Contemporary membership books and accounts demonstrate continuing activity rather than a modern association-football code.

**What actually happened:** Collected subscriptions; procured balls, bladders, leather casings and goal markers; leased grounds; paid someone to assist in setup; retained 1824–25 and 1831–32 member lists. Particularly relevant to the attendance problem: Hope **categorised members according to which days they could attend**, regulating numbers ahead of games. A handwritten note on playing rules from **1833** survives; earlier shorthand saying the rules were entirely unknown is too strong, but the records do not justify reconstructing modern soccer.

**Mechanism:** Membership network + pooled money + scheduling + equipment management made recurring recreation possible. We have a documented response to availability, **not** evidence of mandatory attendance or penalties for no-shows.

**Evidence:** [Scottish Football Museum, Scotland's Football Origins](https://www.scottishfootballmuseum.org.uk/football-for-all/scotlands-football-origins/) (1824 entry); [National Records of Scotland archive showcase](https://blog.nrscotland.gov.uk/2018/04/09/trailblazers-the-worlds-first-football-club-with-john-hutchinson-andy-mitchell/). The detailed archives GD253 are held by NRS; not all primary material is fully reproduced online.

### Case B — Cambridge football-playing students (1840s–1863)

**Who/why:** Students accustomed to different school forms of football wanted to play with others at Cambridge. A practical difficulty was differing conceptions of permissible handling, offside, physical contact, etc.

**What actually happened:** An 1848 Cambridge compromise rule set is described in an **1897 retrospective letter**; the original 1848 code has **not survived**. A later printed university code probably dates to about 1856, and a revised Cambridge code from 1863 survives. This is good evidence of local coordination over the *game*, but not enough to assert exactly how a particular 1848 session worked or that everyone adopted the same code.

**Mechanism:** Agreement on a play code can solve an activity-level problem without an external governing body. Do not treat 1848 as the undisputed invention of association football.

**Evidence:** [Cambridge University Library Special Collections](https://specialcollections-blog.lib.cam.ac.uk/?p=5891); [Sheffield club archival catalogue, discussion of Cambridge rules](https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2011/english-literature-history-childrens-books-illustrations-l11404/lot.10.html).

### Case C — Sheffield FC (1857–1859)

**Who/why:** A group of educated Sheffield professional men (solicitors, manufacturers, merchants, doctors) wanted regular football play and sporting/social activities. Prest had links to cricket, but the contemporary files do **not** establish that Sheffield FC's sole founding purpose was off-season cricket training; later 1907 reminiscences emphasise lack of opportunities for ex-school men to keep playing in leisure time.

**What actually happened:** Club had approximately 62 members by the end of its first year, subscriptions, officers, committee, sports day and prizes, audited accounts, 6 footballs costing £2 6s, beer and other expenditure. Football initially played internally, with various team divisions; games were not necessarily eleven-a-side. Minute books distinguish **club governance rules** from **laws of the game** and show debate and revisions at October 1858 meetings. 1859 printed code circulated to members.

**Mechanism:** A continuing body can acquire shared property, organise members, cover costs and enact decisions; creating such a body did not require existing opponents or competitions. Do not jump from 'formed club' to 'needed an interclub league'.

**Evidence:** [Sotheby's 2011 catalogue summarising original minutes, account books and 1859 printings](https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2011/english-literature-history-childrens-books-illustrations-l11404/lot.10.html); [Sotheby's 2021 1859 rules pamphlet](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2021/books-and-manuscripts-19th-and-20th-century/sheffield-football-club-rules-regulations-laws).

### Case D — Blackheath (1858–1862)

**Who/why:** Former pupils wanted to continue the rugby-like game they played at Blackheath Proprietary School.

**What actually happened:** The club's history explicitly says they were **not a formal club with officers in 1858**; they initially played ad hoc and had no secretary or treasurer until 1862. They attempted to field an ex-pupils side, but did not have enough former pupils available and repeatedly brought in outsiders. They formally opened participation, calling themselves Blackheath FC from the 1862–63 season.

**Mechanism:** Solve thin attendance by **broadening recruitment eligibility**, rather than paying players or establishing a competition. Informal playing group preceded formally constituted club. Precise foundation date is contested because surviving early written records are thin.

**Evidence:** [Blackheath Rugby's detailed club history, admits dates uncertain](https://blackheathrugby.co.uk/our-history/). Retrospective institutional account; avoid attributing undocumented motives to particular members.

### Case E — Barnes FC (1862): club as infrastructure rather than compulsory team

**Who/why:** Ebenezer Cobb Morley, an all-round sportsman who joined the London Rowing Club in Barnes, founded Barnes FC with friends and rowing colleagues. Later records and surviving 1862 club rules permit unusually direct examination of operating arrangements.

**What actually happened:** Annual **1 shilling** dues; captain, secretary/treasurer and 7-person committee; admission on introduction by two members subject to committee approval; **friends of members and any Barnes parish resident allowed to play unless the captain forbade it**; Barnes Green named as place for play; season October to April; **balls and club property held at White Hart public house and any four members permitted to borrow them** subject to club rules. Fine of **one shilling for a wilful rule violation**, **not** a documented no-show fine. Disputes decided by captain with appeal to committee. Playing laws appear separately from club governance rules.

**Mechanism:** The formal club pooled and managed resources and granted access, while small groups could play without assembling an official representative team every time. Inclusive guest rule broadened prospective participant pool. Do not assume those rules were rigorously enforced or that attendance was the reason they were adopted.

**Evidence:** [Transcription of Barnes club's 1862 rules](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Rules_of_Barnes_Football_Club_%281862%29) (Wikisource credits a 1951 printed reproduction, G.W. Keeton, *The Soccer Club Secretary*, pp. 162–163); [Barnes club history](https://barnesfootballclub.co.uk/club-info/clubhistory); [Hull History Centre: Morley's rowing connections](https://hullhistorycentre.org.uk/research/research-guides/ebenezer-cobb-morley.aspx). The early rules' provenance is a transcription of a secondary printing; ideally locate 1862 original before publishing a verbatim claim.

### Case F — Richmond group, 1863: football without a permanent club

**Who/why:** A temporary group, with many participants training for army careers and unlikely to stay in Richmond.

**What actually happened:** Edwin H. Ash explained in a letter of 28 November 1863 (published contemporaneously in *The Sporting Life*) that the group was **not an organised club**, but met during the winter to play a few matches. He judged forming a permanent club inappropriate for a group that might disappear after the season, although they could still adopt other people's playing rules for convenience.

**Mechanism:** A recurring activity or temporary representative 'team' does not require a lasting membership institution when anticipated lifespan is short. **Counterexample to treating formal clubs as necessary or as the only rational solution.**

**Evidence:** [*The Sporting Life*, 1863, fourth meeting report, letter from Edwin H. Ash](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Sporting_Life/1863/Football_Association_Meeting) (contemporary newspaper report, later transcription). Mention of the meeting is source provenance, **not** a reason to advance the investigation to FA governance.

### Case G — Hallam FC (1860): branch of a pre-existing sporting organisation

**Who/why:** Hallam Cricket Club had over 300 members by the 1850s. According to its later official history, some wanted a less exclusive football-playing alternative to Sheffield FC. It began football in 1860 using facilities associated with the cricket club.

**What happened:** Members of an existing recreation organisation organised a football activity; played local opposition. The founding claim should be treated as a retrospective club account. The cause and terms of the first fixture need confirmation with contemporary records before stronger causal claims.

**Mechanism:** Organisations can **add** a football section without first building an independent institution from scratch.

**Evidence:** [Hallam FC historical account](https://hallamfc.co.uk/our-history/); [Sotheby's Sheffield archive contains 1907 Shaw letters — retrospective](https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2011/english-literature-history-childrens-books-illustrations-l11404/lot.10.html).

### Case H — Melbourne footballers (1858–1859): a distinct code abroad

**Who/why:** Tom Wills's **contemporary July 1858 letter** proposed winter football for cricket players, including a club and committee to formulate playing laws. Students and cricketers had already begun organising matches, but groups brought different playing traditions.

**What happened:** Informal scratch and school matches took place in 1858. After a May 1859 scratch match, interested gentlemen met at the Parade Hotel, elected officers and a committee, then wrote the Melbourne Football Club's own ten rules. Its game evolved towards Australian rules football, **not** modern association football. In 1860 and 1861 players could sometimes play for supposed rival teams, indicating identities and membership were not invariably rigid.

**Mechanism:** Recreational groups can deliberately establish club administration and a playing code to coordinate participation and game design. Similar problems can lead to different football codes rather than one inevitable solution.

**Evidence:** [National Museum of Australia](https://www.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/resources/australian-rules-football); [Melbourne FC's detailed 1858–61 timeline](https://www.melbournefc.com.au/news/270450/celebrating-160-years-mfc-snapshot-part-one); [AFL discussion of 1859 playing rules](https://www.afl.com.au/news/133990/when-australian-football-was-born-these-were-the-first-10-rules).

### Cross-case findings (bounded)

1. **Attendance:** Edinburgh used day-of-week availability groupings; Blackheath widened its eligible player pool; Barnes permitted non-members to play. Three *different* documented responses to related coordination issues.
2. **Resources:** Shared equipment and facility access can justify a continuing membership organisation even if games themselves are informal: Edinburgh, Sheffield and Barnes.
3. **Governance:** A member club can fix contributions, keep common property, appoint decision-makers and define who may join. This is distinct from the laws governing play.
4. **Non-club alternative:** Richmond's winter group explicitly saw no value in formalising an organisation whose membership was temporary. A football game or team need not be a club.
5. **Origins are not universal:** Cambridge drew on varied school games; Melbourne wrote distinct rules; Sheffield's specific leisure needs should not be projected onto the rest.
6. **Internal vs external competition:** Sheffield could organise play among its members, while Hallam built on an existing cricket organisation. The *reason* to organise an outside fixture, rather than simply enlarge an internal pool, remains an open question.
7. **Money was present, but not automatically commercial:** Shared subscriptions, equipment and ground hire are demonstrated. They do not imply paying footballers or selling admission.

### Limitations / next primary-source tasks

- Direct primary inspection of Hope Papers, particularly the 1833 rules and day-of-week groupings, to establish exactly how matches were arranged.
- Find original or reliably contemporary printing of 1862 Barnes club rules; currently accessed via a mid-20th-century reproduction.
- Examine Sheffield 1858–60 minutes for attendance, selection, recruitment, guests and internal fixture arrangements (catalogue alone does not show every detail).
- Find contemporary Blackheath membership / match records; present explanation is club retrospection and founding date not entirely settled.
- Read Cambridge's surviving 1856-ish and 1863 code plus contemporary student documents; 1848 version not extant.
- Confirm Hallam's origins against 1860 newspapers and cricket club minutes rather than only contemporary club website.
- Avoid conflating team sizes, codes, and the modern meaning of 'club'; several groups played rugby-like or Australian variants.
- Do not conclude a particular organizational arrangement 'caused' the later FA, professional competition, or modern game's attractiveness. Such arguments require a separate, later study.

**Editorial guardrail:** We have a genuinely informative comparison, but not yet a sports feature. Ask 'Where's the consequence?' only when the consequences are supported. The historical evidence is a tool for discovering the game, not a task to build an exhaustive family tree.

## Why play outsiders if your own game already works? (evidence check, 19 October 2026)

**Starting question:** What changed between 'we have our game and enough people' and 'we want to play someone else'? **Do not assume a breakdown or need for universal rules.** External matches were an elective additional benefit, not a universal necessity. Some school house or internal club contests remained sufficient.

### Sheffield: unusual direct recollection of why outside opponents appealed

- 1857: Sheffield FC was founded to provide football among its own members. It assembled internal teams using married/unmarried status, surname halves, occupations, etc. Those games were possible; the club already had rules.
- **December 1858:** Sheffield played army-regiment opposition, its first recorded outside fixture. National Football Museum reports the 84th Regiment in December 1858, then the 58th Regiment shortly before Hallam 1860; secondary sources sometimes conflate which regiment came first — verify in original press.
- **September 1860:** Hallam FC was formed by John Charles Shaw and Thomas Edward Vickers with links to Hallam Cricket Club and the Sheffield playing group; a distinct organisation with cricket-club membership and ground.
- **26 December 1860:** First Sheffield–Hallam inter-club fixture at Sandygate, about four miles from Sheffield's original site, under Sheffield rules.
- **18 October 1907:** Hallam cofounder and former Sheffield member John Charles Shaw wrote to J.C. Bingham that Sheffield's internal teams had been rearranged to keep matches interesting; he founded Hallam, persuading some Sheffield players to join, **'for the purpose of getting a team to play against the old club'**; remembered resulting encounters as '**most interesting & enjoyable**'. He also recalled prior matches with regiments. Direct participant testimony, **but late retrospective testimony** nearly 50 years after events. The 'for many years' phrasing requires caution as Sheffield FC had existed only three years before Hallam; early informal play may be included, or memory may be imprecise.
- **December 1862:** Contemporary newspaper accounts in the Sheffield archive described intensely partisan supporters of Hallam and disputed on-field violence, with correspondence contesting blame. This is evidence that **durable club identities** could make contests emotionally different from arbitrarily mixed teams; it **does not prove this was the original motive** or that rivalry required separate clubs.
- **By 1862:** Sotheby's archive catalogue says eleven teams were active in Sheffield region playing Sheffield rules and reports regular external fixtures, so outside contest proliferated *without* a national rule code. This is a bounded example, not a national trend estimate.

**Sources:** [Sotheby's catalogue, 2011, direct archival descriptions and verbatim extracts of Shaw's 1907 letter, 1862 paper](https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2011/english-literature-history-childrens-books-illustrations-l11404/lot.10.html); [National Football Museum, 1860 Rules Derby](https://nationalfootballmuseum.com/stories/12-games-1860/); [Hallam official history](https://hallamfc.co.uk/our-history/).

### Outside opposition was already an option elsewhere

- **Schools:** Interschool and old-boys' football fixtures existed before 1863 (e.g. school vs school or old boys against another former-pupil group). Many school groups still preferred internal competitions. Need contemporary correspondence to know precisely why any given first fixture was desired. Their cricket cultures already featured school-vs-school sporting contests, e.g. Eton–Harrow played cricket at Lord's in **1805**, showing external challenges weren't a novel concept invented in football. [Lord's history](https://www.lords.org/lords/our-history/father-time-wall/1805-the-first-eton-v-harrow-match-takes-place-at).
- **Melbourne:** Melbourne Grammar vs Scotch College played a widely publicised 40-a-side football match starting **7 August 1858**, resumed for further sessions, ultimately drawn, before Melbourne's 1859 code. These were independently identifiable school sides; arranging the match preceded fully codifying their *own* regional game. Evidence for the match is clear; the personal motive of the initiators should not be inferred simply from the fact of playing. [National Museum of Australia](https://www.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/resources/australian-rules-football); [AFL history](https://www.afl.com.au/news/56804/a-game-is-born).
- **University groups:** Students from schools with differing football traditions could share a place and want to play despite distinct rule systems. Agreeing a local playing code was one optional solution. Original Cambridge 1848 rule text not extant; later versions and retrospective accounts exist. [Sotheby's Cambridge note](https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2011/english-literature-history-childrens-books-illustrations-l11404/lot.10.html).

### Interpretive distinction: what did they gain?

**Directly attested for Sheffield/Hallam by participant recollection:** internal permutations ceased to satisfy at least some players' desire for *interesting* contests; an outside club was deliberately assembled as opposition.

**Plausible benefits to test, not blanket proven reasons:** unfamiliar opponents and play styles, pride in distinct group identities, enduring rivalry, proving which group could win, social occasions, publicity, spectators. A regular internal house/team tournament could provide some of these; therefore separate clubs were *not logically required*.

**Do not claim:** Clubs universally outgrew internal matches, outsiders were intrinsically better, the two sides required national rules, school football suddenly became unviable, or the FA was a necessary outcome. Distinguish desire for outside fixtures **from** any subsequent need to negotiate the laws of a particular fixture.

### New working inference

Early club football may have spread partly because an **outside opponent was a new recreational opportunity**, not because the original activity ceased working. In one unusually documented case, a founder deliberately **created** opposition. Opponent supply and intergroup identity are separate from standardisation. Track further cases of invite/challenge records, especially groups refusing outside matches despite ample membership.

## How clubs began selecting representative players (evidence checked 19 October 2026)

**Prompt:** Did external fixtures create competition for selection, and what happened to members who did not make the side? Do not project modern 'first eleven', mandatory eleven-a-side or merit selection back onto 1850s football.

### Sheffield v Hallam, 26 December 1860

The Boxing Day 1860 match was **advertised as sixteen-a-side**, but a contemporary *Sheffield Daily Telegraph* match report (27 December 1860) states that severe weather caused absences on both sides, and that **Sheffield played with fewer men than Hallam and won 2–0**. The actual on-field numbers are not securely established by this report; do **not** describe it as an actual 16-v-16 match. The opponents were advertised as **Hallam and Stumperlowe**, captained by John Charles Shaw, against Sheffield captained by Nathaniel Creswick. This establishes representative playing groups, but **not the selection procedure or a fixed 16-man team**. Evidence: [transcription of *Sheffield Daily Telegraph*, 27 December 1860](https://spartacus-educational.com/FsheffieldA.htm); [historical notice from 22 December 1860 discussed in *Origin Stories* sample](https://www.pitchpublishing.co.uk/sites/default/files/associated_downloads/Origin%20Stories%20SAMPLE.pdf); [Hallam history](https://hallamfc.co.uk/our-history/).

Evidence: [Penistone Church FC's historical account](https://www.penistonechurchfc.com/history), [Hallam FC history](https://hallamfc.co.uk/our-history/). Locate underlying Sheffield Daily Telegraph report before stronger claims.

### Barnes (1862–63): flexible players and numbers

Barnes' 1862 club rules permitted members' friends and Barnes parish residents to participate unless the captain objected. No identified formal best-eleven selection rules. [Barnes 1862 rules transcription](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Rules_of_Barnes_Football_Club_(1862)).

December 12 **1863**, Forest v Barnes had been arranged **15-a-side**, but only **12 Forest** and **13 Barnes** players appeared, owing to circumstances; they played. Contemporary *Bell's Life* match report published Dec 19 transcribed [here](https://playupliverpool.com/1863/12/12/the-forest-football-club-adapts-the-fa-rules/). **Attendance shortfalls were still a major match-level practical issue**, not proof of an oversubscribed merit ladder. Do not confuse the report's commentator's pro-FA editorial with the players' motives.

### Queen's Park (founded 1867): explicit pathway from membership games to selection hierarchy

The *History of the Queen's Park Football Club, 1867–1917*, by Richard Robinson (published circa 1920, based on club minutes), records:

- Founding constitution (9 August 1867): **'the recreation and amusement of its members'**. Football played internally regularly; early senior outside fixtures scarce. See [Chapter LXII](https://electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter62.htm) and [Chapter VII](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter7.htm).
- Club's annual meeting **5 April 1872**: captain reported that a **Second Eleven** had played Southern FC during 1871–72 season and won **2–0**. Club elected a separate Second Eleven captain empowered with first-team captain to arrange outside matches. Its second XI had its own fixtures and soon served as supply of players to first team. [Chapter XLVI](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter46.htm).
- Selection was initially concentrated in club captain. Author states first **13 years** captain judged merits and controlled senior side, and vice-captain selected opposition in internal practices. [Chapter III](https://electricscotland.com/History/sport/football/chapter3.htm).
- Second Eleven players were invited into first team as vacancies arose, given trials and sometimes retained permanently. On **20 October 1877**, **George Ker** from Second XI was tried as full-back against Third Lanark and received praise (see [Chapter XXX](https://www.electricscotland.com/hiStory/sport/football/chapter30.htm); and [Chapter XIV](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter14.htm)). Author says ability was decisive, but notes personal history and experience affected choices, so treat pure meritocracy as *historian's interpretation*, not unimpeachable contemporary finding.
- **31 August 1875**: committee recommended getting up a **Third Eleven** if practicable.
- **25 July 1878**: W. C. Mitchell proposed a Third Eleven, the **Hampden Eleven**, **specifically to keep together players unsuccessful in gaining places in the Second Eleven**; committee approved. **This is a direct documentary response to the very exclusion/participation problem raised by the user.** [Chapter XLVI](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter46.htm).
- **29 April 1880**: club abolished annually elected captains and transferred team selection and fixture arrangements to a match committee. [Chapter III](https://electricscotland.com/History/sport/football/chapter3.htm).
- **7 October 1871** against Granville, **six Queen's Park members reportedly played for Granville**. Even with outside matches, football team boundaries could be fluid and opponents lent players to make up numbers. [Chapter VII](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter7.htm). Note discrepancy across sources on exact score and dates; preserve broad evidence of borrowing players, verify newspaper before publishing.

**Interpretation:** Competition for a recognised first team **coexisted** with the original recreational club; the consequence for non-selected members was not necessarily expulsion or inability to play. Second and third teams could be created under the *same institution*, preserving overall identity but introducing a playing hierarchy. Internal practice and flexible scratch matches persisted. This is evidence of progression **in at least one club**, not a demonstration that every club did so or that outside fixtures alone caused merit selection. The cup competitions and desire to maintain reputation may have intensified selection, but further comparative evidence is required to assign relative causal weight.

### Source quality / next verification

Robinson (1920) draws upon original club minutes but also supplies hindsight and interpretations; quoted minute dates and motions are stronger evidence than general claims about motivations. Identify and consult original Queen's Park minutes or earliest press to verify precise wording. Sheffield's 1860 lineup **does not** establish first-eleven merit selection; avoid that leap. Preserve contemporary match report about Barnes and Forest playing short-handed.

**Finding:** Outside opposition creates *an opportunity* for inter-club representation and intra-club selection, but not an automatic move to eleven elite players. Queen's Park supplies unusually direct evidence that, where hierarchy appeared, clubs could expand into second and third teams instead of permanently dividing the club into separate rivals.

## Incentives first — player values, outside options, and trade-offs (19 October 2026)

**Status:** Research on **Incentive 1: Why play football at all?**; NOT a story outline. Any previous football article draft is premature and shelved while incentives are analysed.

### Distinguish three actors before making claims

1. **People who enjoy playing football**: activity itself is the goal, possibly particular tactics, competition, status, social enjoyment, physical challenge.
2. **People using football for another goal**: e.g. fitness for cricket or social participation; football can be replaced by alternatives.
3. **People wanting others to play**: e.g. school organisers promoting attendance, clubs seeking members; organisers' incentives are not each individual player's motives.

**Central test:** What does the individual value? What can they do instead? What feature of their existing game are they prepared to lose to get more opportunities, opponents or recognition?

### Strong, sourced actor statements

**Etonensis (pseudonym), London Daily News, 6 Oct 1863** (historical abstract of contemporaneous letter, *not yet full original reproduction*): Argued that incompatible school codes prevented outsiders' fixtures **and** a player being recognised for skill beyond his existing associates. Described two years of Eton–Westminster compromise matches as technically unsatisfactory because neither could use favourite methods; nevertheless advocated uniform rules. Thus an identifiable **status/recognition incentive** could compete against **enjoyment of specialised skills and familiar code**. [Play Up Liverpool summary of original newspaper item](https://playupliverpool.com/1863/10/06/lets-frame-rules-for-one-universal-game/). **Do not represent the site's summary as verbatim original letter.**

**F. M. Campbell, Blackheath, Bell's Life report of 1 Dec 1863:** Identified running with ball, hacking/physical challenge, pluck and courage as *constitutive* of the game he valued. Threatened withdrawal if rules removed those activities, saying their restrictions meant opponents could play 'our game in your way' but the reverse was impossible. Gives **revealed willingness to forego wider association** to preserve valued rules. Distinguish what he believed others liked from personal testimony of those other players. [Full historical press transcription](https://playupliverpool.com/1863/12/01/the-fourth-meeting-of-the-football-association/), especially Campbell speech and Morley response.

**W. C. Morley, same debate, Dec 1863:** Expected adult London clubs, including men in business, to avoid football if 'hacking' risk were compulsory. His own words say he did not personally mind hacking so much, but sought rules that would promote adoption among potential players. This is **organiser's estimate of other players' willingness to participate**, not direct survey of the players. [Same newspaper report](https://playupliverpool.com/1863/12/01/the-fourth-meeting-of-the-football-association/).

**Eton boy quoted in school archive (date of original comment not established by web article):** Defended Eton Field Game as a cherished institution and argued soccer passing permitted idle players to avoid physical exertion. Indicates **valued identity and vigorous participation**, not merely abstract attachment to rules. But **do not date this to 1863** until original quotation located and dated. [Eton College collections archivist article](https://collections.etoncollege.com/the-origins-of-the-field-game/), see also [archival sports exhibition PDF](https://collections.etoncollege.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2025/01/Sport_Eton_Catalogue.pdf).

**Queen's Park, Glasgow, founding constitution 9 Aug 1867:** Explicit goal 'recreation and amusement of its members.' This **establishes collective declared organisational purpose**, not each individual's personal motive. Later historian Robinson also recounts pre-club scratch recreation, variable attendance and sometimes inviting bystanders to fill numbers. [Robinson's club history chapter XI](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter11.htm) and [chapter II](https://www.electricscotland.com/History/sport/football/chapter2.htm). **Post-1863 case**; useful for general incentive study, not as cause of FA's formation.

**Tom Wills, original letter published Bell's Life in Victoria, 10 Jul 1858:** Offered football to solve **winter fitness/inactivity** among cricketers, avoiding weight gain and perhaps improving cricket turf. Explicitly proposed **rifle club or other athletic games** if football impractical. The motivational actor is *Wills proposing a facility/activity for others*; not proof individual Melbourne cricketers all valued football over those alternatives. [Letter transcription](https://www.australianculture.org/winter-practice-1858/).

**Rugby School's laws, approved 28 Aug 1845:** Provide punitive mechanisms for nonattendance without acceptable excuse, obligatory attendance after other duties, organised announcement of matches and old pupils' participation with permission. This shows some people might play **because attendance was expected/enforced**, not just because they personally enjoyed it. Those are school regulations, NOT a confession of what pupils wanted. [Original printed laws transcription](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Laws_of_Football_as_played_at_Rugby_School_(1845)).

### Motivation vs alternatives table (interpretive, bounded)

| Who / position | Value as attested | Alternatives reasonably open to them | What could be sacrificed? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Etonensis 1863 | Show ability beyond familiar school circle; find wider opposition | Play only colleagues, keep occasional compromise games, learn other code, advocate a common code | Valued school-specific technique; weak compromise could degrade play | Contemporary correspondent, summarised by later website |
| Blackheath's Campbell 1863 | Running/carrying, physical challenge, courage and 'pluck' | Accept compromise, play only aligned clubs, seek separate organisation | Access to a broader rule-sharing network if refuses | Contemporary newspaper transcription; his own speech |
| Eton pupil, undated in archive | Tradition; energetic, hard-to-evade participation | Try association football or remain in Eton game | Identity and specific challenge of Field Game | Archivist quotes pupil; original quote date not established |
| Queen's Park committee 1867 | Recreation and amusement for members | Informal scratch play or continuing membership club | Time, dues, organisational effort | Founding constitution quoted in later history |
| Tom Wills 1858 | Winter fitness / benefit for cricketers | Football, rifle club, other athletic games | Ability to achieve aims with different activity if football impractical | Contemporary written public proposal |
| Rugby school organisers 1845 | Reliable attendance in scheduled games | Enforced participation, regulations and absence exceptions | Some pupils' freedom to opt out | Contemporary rules; **not** evidence about a pupil's underlying enjoyment |

### What this permits us to conclude

1. 'Likes football' is incomplete: players can value **particular physical mechanics, traditions, skill expression, challenge or recognition** differently.
2. 'More opponents' is **not unconditionally desirable**: if it requires removing what made the game enjoyable, a rational player might decline.
3. A player can want **wider recognition** enough to prefer a code that worsens their present playing experience (Etonensis example).
4. **Health/fitness** is a substitutable objective (Wills names other sports), whereas attachment to a particular football code may not be.
5. **Participation itself** can be partly compelled (Rugby 1845), so attendance is not proof of enthusiasm.
6. An organiser's theory of participant behaviour (Morley, Wills) is not direct individual player testimony.
7. **Evidence gap:** No representative dataset tells us how common each incentive was; avoid frequency estimates or 'everyone' statements.

- Date and inspect **Eton's quoted pupil criticism of association passing**, avoid anachronism.
- Collect first-person reasons for joining specific early clubs (not just club constitutional objectives).
- Keep separate **what they liked about a game** from **what they wanted from organised competition**.
- Only then model the decision to form a club or play outside opponents, and investigate the outcomes. **No new article draft yet.**

## Incentive 2 — 'I want to play football: how do I get a game?' (19 October 2026)

**Status:** Sufficient preliminary evidence for organisational alternatives, not yet a causal ranking. Do not write a new article yet. Do not assume a formal club, an opponent outside the group, a fixed XI, national playing laws or playing for a fee.

### Feasible choices, assessed from the individual player's perspective

| Choice | Potential advantage | Potential costs or barriers | Evidence / status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Return to school or former-pupil games | Familiar players, facilities, code | Access permission, restricted calendar, school membership | Rugby School laws (1845) allow former pupils with captains' approval; do not claim universal permission elsewhere |
| Informal scratch games | No institutional maintenance overhead, use existing friends | Need recruitment and place/time for each fixture | Richmond 1863 explicitly preferred playing occasional winter fixtures without long-lived club (contemporary letter); Blackheath began ad hoc |
| Join existing local football group or club | Existing player pool and arrangements | Admissions, dues, fit of preferred rules, geography | Barnes 1862: 1s annual subscription, admission via two-member sponsorship and committee approval; friends/local residents admitted to play with captain's permission |
| Add football to an existing institution | Already have facilities / membership network | Need agreement from other members, available grounds and time | Hallam Cricket Club's football section; relative motivations depend on later club retrospective |
| Establish a club | Continuing recruitment, membership continuity, pooled equipment/grounds, agreed decision process | Upfront recruitment, time for administration, regular money and attendance obligations | John Hope's Edinburgh club retained subscriptions, ground receipts and ball expenses; Sheffield club 1857–59 record officers, balls and accounts |
| Stop or substitute another activity | Avoid cost and difficulty of organising matches | Lose some football-specific enjoyment | A logical option; Tom Wills (1858) explicitly regarded other physical activities as substitutes for fitness but this is Incentive 1 |

**Don't conflate:** A game being held; a temporary team acting under a name; a membership institution; a representative team.

### First-hand/historical contrasts

**Richmond, November 1863: explicit choice *not* to create a formal club.** Edwin H. Ash wrote that the group was mostly temporary men preparing for the army and unlikely to remain long in Richmond. They formed a group in winter to play occasional matches, and a lasting membership organisation was not worth establishing. Quoting a contemporary letter as transcribed by Wikisource from *The Sporting Life*: 'it is useless our trying to form a regular club'. The letter also envisaged adopting common laws for convenience, showing that an informal team could choose common rules without being a formal club. *Do not infer the FA was required to make their matches happen.* https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Sporting_Life/1863/Football_Association_Meeting

**Blackheath, 1858–1862: the player pool changed before formal organisation.** Blackheath's club retrospective notes no secretary/treasurer until 1862 and ad hoc early games. Former pupils could not supply enough available players, so invited outsiders. They opened membership by 1862–63. Distinguish the original school game and the eventual more formal club. https://blackheathrugby.co.uk/our-history/

**Barnes, 1862: a formal club enabling informal play.** Printed regulations (extant in later transcription) say: annually paid members (1 shilling), two-member introductions plus committee approval, local residents and members' friends permitted to play subject to captain's decision, Barnes Green as venue, balls stored at White Hart and **any four members permitted to borrow club property for use**. This indicates club as **shared access/infrastructure**, not a compulsory formal representative XI for each session. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Rules_of_Barnes_Football_Club_(1862)

**John Hope's Edinburgh Foot-Ball Club (1824–1841): pooled resources for sustained activity.** National Records Scotland describes/illustrates subscriptions, Dalry Park hire receipt 1826 and ball inflation expense 1832. This proves practical club running costs, not modern code or profit motivation. https://blog.nrscotland.gov.uk/2018/04/09/trailblazers-the-worlds-first-football-club-with-john-hutchinson-andy-mitchell/

**Sheffield (1857–1859): why formalising might be worth effort.** Original minute books and 1859 printed code record annual sports, subscriptions, elected officers, balls bought and club game laws. Contemporary archive accounts indicate activity and shared property; **1907 jubilee history** claims adults formerly familiar with school football lacked opportunities to pursue it in leisure. This latter account is retrospective. 1858 rules drafted *after* play had begun. https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2011/english-literature-history-childrens-books-illustrations-l11404/lot.10.html ; https://shefflibraries.blogspot.com/2018/10/the-sheffield-football-rules-of-1858.html

### Interim incentive findings

- If friends, opponents, place and mutually acceptable rules are already available, a **permanent club is optional**: Richmond shows conscious choice not to form one; early Blackheath shows informal-to-formal progression.
- A club may be worthwhile where members expect to keep playing and need **reliable administration or shared resources**: Edinburgh, Sheffield and Barnes document subscriptions, equipment and venues. That is a plausible **mechanism**, not proven unique motivation for every club.
- Club membership need not be identical to eligibility to play: Barnes admitted guests/parish residents; Blackheath widened potential players.
- Opening a new club is only one possible response to shortage of players; relaxing eligibility or using an existing club can work.
- Club formation may reflect **who is already connected** (former pupils, cricket members, professional men), not necessarily a desire to create an exclusive team or inter-club championship.

### Scope limit

These records don't identify how common each option was, or determine the exact attendance threshold or member cost at which club formation becomes rational. Do not mathematically model an attendance problem unless a specific documented decision requires it.

**Next decision (Incentive 3):** Once I can get a game regularly, what would induce me to invest in a permanent club, or to arrange fixtures with other groups? Treat these as **two distinct choices**, not automatic stages of one process.

## Incentive 3 — 'Our club can already play: why seek outside opponents?' (19 October 2026)

**Core premise:** Remaining an internally-playing recreational club is a viable end state. Outside fixtures are a *choice*; they require an incentive that outweighs costs and any sacrifice to a valued code. No claim that all clubs had such an incentive. Do not draft an article until the incentive analysis is sufficiently grounded.

### Candidate incentives and their status

| Candidate benefit | Documented behaviour or stated reason | Confidence / caution |
|---|---|---|
| More interesting opposition; greater variety | Sheffield's John Charles Shaw later recalled dividing members alphabetically to keep play interesting, then creating Hallam to have opponents for Sheffield; judged those fixtures enjoyable | Strong evidence of his retrospective *claimed motive* (1907 letter) but nearly 50 years' gap; not proof of universal mechanism |
| Improve club's challenge, vigour, standard | **Queen's Park April 1869 minutes**, quoted by Richard Robinson, discussed other clubs to 'stimulate and rouse the general stamina of the club'; captain Robert Gardner in **April 1872** regretted absence of outsiders to test the 'metal' of Queen's Park | Specific committee/club motives, presented in 1920 club history using dated minutes; more direct than an inference of boredom |
| Represent one's group, test relative ability and claim status | Queen's Park 1869 match victory inspired H. N. Smith's celebratory poem, printed and distributed by the club; Sheffield v Hallam 1862 reports passionate crowd reactions | Evidence of meaning *after* external contests, not conclusive evidence of the original desire to arrange them |
| Seek chances for selection | Selection and second/third XIs clearly emerge at Queen's Park by the 1870s | A consequence and possibly secondary incentive, but not directly established as reason for arranging the earliest outside matches |
| Socialising, travel or adventure | Outside fixtures created visits to other clubs | Plausible, but no verified early direct evidence identifying these as dominant participant motives; sometimes travel was a deterrent |

### Concrete 1867–72 Queen's Park choices: benefits weighed against costs

**Club start:** Original constitution of 9 Aug 1867 described 'recreation and amusement of its members'. The club arranged internal games, had rules, no requirement for a national competition. [Robinson, Ch. XI](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter11.htm).

**1 Aug 1868 — Accept Thistle challenge:** Club played against Thistle under locally negotiated arrangements. A real original letter by club secretary Robert Gardner, dated **29 July 1868**, survives in Scottish Football Museum: it agrees **20 a side**, proposes **two hours** as adequate given the weather, and asks to change ends to balance ground and wind. 2–0 victory. This shows game details could be negotiated *without* a universal rule set, while enjoying outside opposition. [Richard McBrearty, history and description of the original letter](https://scottishfootballorigins.org/2021/04/05/the-story-of-1868-part-one-the-association-game-in-scotland/).

**1868 — Decline Ayr:** Queen's Park also received a challenge from Ayr but rejected an immediate away fixture, according to Robinson, because members would personally pay rail fares, suitable low-cost return travel on a Saturday was impractical, and enough members might not be available in holiday season. Thistle was close enough to play. An outside opponent was attractive, but not *at any price*. [Robinson, Ch. VII](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter7.htm).

**Apr 1869 — Seek more outside games:** A club-minute discussion encouraged challenges to Hamilton, Greenock and Motherwell for the documented purpose of 'stimulat[ing] and rous[ing] the general stamina of the club'. Queen's Park secretary authorised to locate and challenge clubs. [Robinson, Ch. VII](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter7.htm).

**1869 — Challenge Rugby club even though they play another code:** Queen's Park approached **Glasgow Academicals** (Rugby), offering to play **20 a side under Rugby rules**. Accies refused on grounds of different code and scheduling difficulties. Queen's Park's members considered a proposal to obtain/adopt Rugby laws alongside their existing Association laws so they could meet Rugby clubs. An extraordinary general meeting voted, with considerable disagreement, to adopt Rugby rules **for October–March**, over an amendment to retain Association rules exclusively. No Rugby-code match is recorded as the result. **Strong example of a group willing to expand its choice of playable games, while its intended opponents were unwilling or unable to reciprocate.** Do not portray as permanent abandonment of Association football, since it was *additional*, and we don't know individual members' motives. [Robinson, Ch. VII](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter7.htm); corroborating overview [Ch. V](https://electricscotland.com/hiStory/sport/football/chapter5.htm). **This case occurred after the English FA's founding (1863)**, demonstrating continuing options rather than explaining the original English FA founding.

**1871 — Airdrie objects and no match:** Queen's Park agreed to an Airdrie challenge, but when Airdrie read the intended laws and discovered handling prohibited, they asked for a change; Queen's Park refused, and **the particular proposed match did not happen**. They later played each other. Rules compatibility was a *transaction cost*, and compromise was optional even when outsiders desired. [Robinson, Ch. VII](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter7.htm).

**April 1872 — No opponents: use internal competition instead:** Captain Robert Gardner expressed regret at lack of external matches to 'test the metal' of the club; Queen's Park arranged a **six-game series, President's Team v Captain's Team**, to maintain interest and develop play. This is a documented **internal substitute** for external challenge rather than proof outside competition was indispensable. [Robinson, Ch. VII](https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter7.htm).

**Constraints:** 1868 letter is original contemporary document described by museum historian; precise "20 a side" and "two hours" verifiable through its depiction; Robinson's 1920 work directly references club minutes and quotes proposals, but we should verify originals before publishing word-for-word historical claims. Robinson also adds his own retrospective language ('palled on the members' and 'gospel'); distinguish this editorial commentary from words in minute books.

### Sheffield contrast

Sheffield's John Charles Shaw letter dated 18 Oct 1907, held in original club archive, says after internal alphabetically arranged contests he helped establish Hallam 'for the purpose of getting a team to play against the old club'. A distinct external opponent increased match interest and rivalry, without new national laws; early Sheffield-Hallam played local Sheffield rules. [Sotheby's catalogued archive](https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2011/english-literature-history-childrens-books-illustrations-l11404/lot.10.html).

### Interim conclusions and where to stop

- **Additional opponents can be desirable even when the original club's recreational problem is solved.** At least some documented clubs actively sought different challenges.
- **The marginal benefit varies by opponent**: local affordable opposition vs costly long-distance travel; familiar rules vs unpleasant code changes.
- **Not everyone has equal desire for compromise**: Queen's Park considered playing Rugby rules; Glasgow Academicals refused; Queen's Park refused Airdrie's demanded handling concession in 1871.
- **Internal contests are viable alternatives** to playing outsiders and need not create permanently separate clubs, as Queen's Park's President-v-Captain series demonstrates.
- **External matches do not imply selection for an elite XI, same team size, a national governing body, or mandatory code convergence.** Negotiation might cover time, side size and ground.
- Nothing here establishes *how widespread each incentive was* or why the FA's code eventually became globally widespread. This is just one decision stage; do not impose later outcomes.

**Next research decision, only once Incentive 3 is accepted:** When an outside match requires a different game or compromises, how do groups decide between changing rules, adapting for a fixture or walking away? Consider differences among *players*, *captains*, and *club committees*, and use the Queen's Park–Accies and Queen's Park–Airdrie cases to inspect revealed preferences.

## Incentive 4: What did clubs really negotiate, concede, reject and abandon? (19 Oct 2026)

**Question:** When clubs wanted an outside match but had divergent laws, what happened *in the actual negotiations*? This is not a narrative of inevitable FA standardisation. Distinguish an **offer**, **accepted terms**, **game played**, **conditions refused**, and **a particular abandoned fixture**.

### 1. Sheffield FC ↔ Notts / Nottingham FC, 1864–1865 — explicit bargains
Sheffield's original 1864–68 committee book, quoted in an archival sale catalogue, records a home-and-away fixture arrangement: **play by Nottingham rules away and Sheffield rules at Sheffield**. Sotheby's catalogue transcribes a meeting date of **17 Dec 1865** but says the match was to be **2 Jan 1865** — internally inconsistent, likely a December *1864* meeting. Verify minute book before quoting precise date. Later history identifies the 2 Jan 1865 away fixture under Nottingham rules (and match numbers vary among secondary histories).

At its **25 Sept 1865 AGM**, Sheffield resolved:
> 'That for the future we play the offside rule, but if the other Sheffd Clubs do not adopt the same rule, we play our Matches with them according to our present rules.'
And separately directed writing to Notts secretary that Sheffield would **adopt an offside rule IF Nottingham abandoned** (i) **'making the mark'** associated with a free kick, and (ii) **the free kick at goal**. These are *minute-recorded proposed conditions*, NOT independently confirmed acceptance by Nottingham. They reveal *selective negotiation of mechanics*, rather than all-or-nothing acceptance of a code. FA/Sheffield offside rules were not necessarily identical.

**Primary record described and transcribed:** https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2011/english-literature-history-childrens-books-illustrations-l11404/lot.10.html .

Also Sheffield's original game ledger reportedly notes **9 May 1863 vs Garrison: 'allowed striking & throwing the ball'**, **28 Oct 1865 vs Mackenzie 'played the offside rules'**, **11 Nov 1865 vs Norton 'played at East Bank to the old rules'**. These are match-specific variations in a single club's practices, transcribed in secondary reference https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheffield_Rules ; verify original match ledger before treating as established direct quotation.

### 2. Queen's Park ↔ Thistle FC, July–August 1868 — logistics and match conditions
A surviving **29 July 1868 original letter** by Queen's Park secretary **Robert Gardner** to Thistle secretary negotiates **20 players per side**, **two-hour duration** appropriate for hot weather and **changing ends** so both sides experience wind and ground fairly. The game went ahead **1 Aug 1868**. Negotiation also concerned size/length/fairness, not only 'carry or kick'. 
**Original artefact described and shown by museum curator Richard McBrearty:** https://scottishfootballorigins.org/2021/04/05/the-story-of-1868-part-one-the-association-game-in-scotland/

### 3. Queen's Park ↔ Drummond FC, 1870 — prohibition on tripping
Robinson's 1920 *History of Queen's Park Football Club* says Drummond favoured **tripping and charging** and Queen's Park **stipulated against tripping**. Their game went ahead 9 July 1870 (16-a-side), and Drummond borrowed two pupils from neighbouring institute to make up their numbers. The record **does not explicitly show acceptance/rejection of charging**; don't say the clubs agreed to abolish it. 
**Source:** https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter7.htm (club history quoting press/minutes).

### 4. Queen's Park ↔ Airdrie, June 1871 — fixture refused over handling
Queen's Park accepted Airdrie's challenge for **22 June 1871**, sent the proposed rules, and Airdrie learned **'hands' were disallowed**. They **refused to play unless Queen's Park deleted the rule**; Queen's Park held firm. **The proposed June 1871 fixture did not take place**. Crucial caveat: same clubs **had played each other in 1870** (several games), and **again on 28 Aug 1872**. Do NOT misrepresent as a permanent boycott or inability ever to play; the *particular fixture* was lost. 
**Source:** https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter7.htm

### 5. Queen's Park ↔ Glasgow Academicals, 1869 — willing to use different code, yet refused
Queen's Park challenged Rugby-playing Glasgow Academicals for a match **under Rugby rules, 20-a-side**. The Academicals declined, citing **rules differences and insufficient time** (their response arrived October). In 1869 Queen's Park then sourced Rugby's laws, had a committee **omit provisions tied to Rugby School's grounds**, and passed an extraordinary meeting motion to **adopt Rugby laws alongside the club's existing association code from October to March**. A minority motion to remain solely with Association laws lost. **No Rugby-code fixture with Academicals or other Rugby club is recorded as a result**. This is internal negotiation of willingness to play another game, not evidence the opponent reciprocated.
**Source:** https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter7.htm

### 6. Sheffield FC ↔ Leeds (Rugby-playing club), 1864 — bespoke hybrid
Sport historian Tony Collins cites Leeds founder J. G. Hudson's retrospective description of their home and away 1864 fixtures under rules of **'a mongrel type, neither rugby nor association'**. Evidence a hybrid code was actually used, but the precise clauses and who requested them **not yet established**.
**Source:** https://tony-collins.squarespace.com/how-football-began-1 . Need original Hudson source citation.

### 7. Hull FC, 1860s — preference to play regardless of code
Tony Collins quotes Hull founder William Hutchinson recalling: **'we played any mortal code possible with other clubs away from home so long as we could get a game of some sort'**. Hull travelled to Lincoln in 1866 to play FA rules despite later being a rugby club. Quote retrospective; trace original Hutchinson memoir before publication.
**Source:** https://tony-collins.squarespace.com/how-football-began-1

### 8. FA / Sheffield disagreement over a return match, 1866–67 (further validation desirable)
After the 31 March 1866 Sheffield–London representative game under FA laws, Sheffield proposed a Sheffield-rules return fixture; FA reportedly agreed to a return fixture **only under FA rules**. A 1867 match was scheduled/postponed over weather, revised rules were disputed, and return ultimately was **not played in 1867**. Can't simply state 'the FA cancelled it over rules': *weather* and scheduling were also factors. Described with citations to press and FA minutes in https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_v_Sheffield_(1866) — pursue original 1866/67 FA minutes before publishing causal claim.

### Interpretation / incentive trade-offs

- **Some wanted match badly enough to change code:** Nottingham home/away; Queen's Park considered Rugby; Hull routinely accommodated outsiders.
- **Some cared enough about particular laws to make a match conditional or refuse:** Sheffield's conditional offside/free-kick offer; Queen's Park no-tripping stipulation; Airdrie refused no-handling and Queen's Park refused deletion; the FA insisted on FA rules for return.
- **Agreement over rules had transaction costs**: circulate code, read and compare laws, committee votes, explicit exceptions, venue-dependent codes, number/duration, scheduling.
- **Refused fixture ≠ permanent hostility**: Queen's Park played Airdrie before and after their failed June 1871 match. Show date and particular match.
- **Attested incentive is not universal**: Some clubs valued access to opponents above code details; others made specific valued actions non-negotiable.
- **A negotiated match is not a universal standard**. These decisions may produce a different match-specific game, not a single national code.

**Next:** Compare the exact **mechanical consequences** of changing one rule — what the game lost/gained under two viable alternatives — and find documented player preference rather than imposing modern judgements. No article premise yet.

## Incentive 4: What changing specific rules did to the game (19 October 2026)

**Research step after negotiation case studies.** Ask: what *activity, tactic, skill or risk* changes under different codes, and what value was the decision-maker hoping to retain or gain? Distinguish (A) mechanics deducible from written laws, (B) **participants' documented preferences**, and (C) actual *experienced* outcomes. No narrative draft and no assumption an FA rule was intrinsically superior.

### A. Offside: the attacking possibilities at stake

- **Sheffield 1858 and 1862 printed laws:** no offside article (absence of a rule in a surviving printed code; note some *unwritten/customary* restrictions may have applied in later matches). By 1863 Sheffield FC had adopted a lenient **one defender** practice, according to contemporaneous secretary Chesterman's letter in Dec 1863 and H. W. Chambers' Feb 1867 retrospective letter to *The Field*. [1858 code](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Sheffield_Rules_(1858)); [1862 code](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Sheffield_Rules_(1862)).
- **FA 1863 Law 6:** a teammate **ahead of the ball** when kicked was out of play, much stricter than later association football offside. This constrains kicks to already advanced teammates and favours moving forward with the ball, dribbling or supporting from behind. The tactical distinction is **deduced from rules**, not direct statistical proof of what footballers actually attempted. [National Football Museum transcription of 13 laws](https://nationalfootballmuseum.com/items/the-laws-of-the-game-1863/).
- **Sheffield AGM 25 Sept 1865:** club **agreed to play a stricter offside rule** with optional old-code games against local clubs who did not adopt it. Conditional offer to Notts FC to keep offside **if Notts abandoned marked kicks and free kicks at goal**. These are **offers / resolutions**; not proof Nottingham accepted. [Original minute transcriptions in Sotheby's archival catalogue](https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2011/english-literature-history-childrens-books-illustrations-l11404/lot.10.html).
- **1865–66 trial outcome:** Sheffield abandoned the strict offside practice. *The Sporting Life*, **30 Jan 1867**, p. 4, article 'Football in Sheffield', reported it was **'universally disapproved of'** and that onside/offside distinctions were very difficult to adjudicate amid excitement, so the prior restriction was restored. The article is quoted by [secondary Sheffield rules chronology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheffield_Rules) citing that exact press issue; **locate original newspaper scan before publication**, and remember newspaper claims like 'universally' cannot establish literal unanimity. H. W. Chambers [The Field, 9 Feb 1867, XXIX(737):104] corroborates descriptions of the local one-defender rule.
- **Material implication:** Agreement with outsiders might expand a club's possible matches **while reducing tactics and creating disputes over what counts as lawful play**. Sheffield's *revealed reversal* is unusually direct empirical evidence of that conflict. Do not confuse one-defender Sheffield offside with current FIFA offside or infer Sheffield introduced offside only in 1865.

### B. Carrying and hacking: what game do players want, and who bears injury risk?

- **FA draft before Dec 1863** Law IX would permit running towards goal carrying the ball from a fair catch or first bounce; Law X then allowed defenders to hold, trip, hack and wrest ball from that ball carrier (but not simultaneously hold and hack). This is a coupled decision: physical defensive tools were justified under the version permitting ball-carrying. [Draft laws](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Laws_of_the_Game_(1863)_(draft)).
- **Adopted FA 1863 Laws 9–12** banned carrying, holding/pushing an adversary and hacking/tripping, **but retained the fair catch and its free kick**. Critical correction to simplified popular story: the original FA game did **not** eliminate catching entirely. [National Football Museum](https://nationalfootballmuseum.com/items/the-laws-of-the-game-1863/).
- **1 Dec 1863 debate, contemporaneous Bell's Life transcript:** Campbell (Blackheath) emphasised 'pluck' and courage from running/hacking, resisted conceding key features, warned removal would render game unacceptable to his people. Morley (Barnes) predicted adult businessmen would refuse a game with hacking due to injury risk; he said he personally didn't mind it much, but prioritised broad participation. [Exact extended contemporaneous debate and recorded vote](https://playupliverpool.com/1863/12/01/the-fourth-meeting-of-the-football-association/).
- **Departure count after FA rules decision (cross-check, 23 Oct 2026):** Eleven clubs joined at the October 1863 founding meeting (Charterhouse's observer is not counted). A tally for January 1864 gives nine members, including four later joiners—Forest School, Sheffield, Uppingham and Royal Engineers—so **six of the original eleven were no longer members** (five originals remained). Chris Lee, *Origin Stories*, names Blackheath, Perceval House and Crusaders among early departures by the time rules were approved. The 1890s contemporary-history book *Football: The Association Game*, ch. II, describes a wider withdrawal of clubs favouring Rugby rules. **Caution:** do not claim all six resigned in protest on 8 December, nor that every club's exit motive is independently proved. Article line should state membership fact, not ascribe individual motives. Sources: https://www.everand.com/book/897500776/Origin-Stories-The-Pioneers-Who-Took-Football-to-the-World ; https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Football:_The_Association_Game/Chapter_2 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Football_Association#Split_from_rugby .

- **Sheffield 1858:** original rules permitted pushing an opponent and hitting/pushing the **ball** with hands, forbade hacking/tripping/holding, **and** permitted a fair catch (but not carrying). Their preference cannot be collapsed into 'all adults want no handling and no contact'. [1858 code](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Sheffield_Rules_(1858)).
- **Mechanics deduction:** A permitted carry enables direct advances in possession with different defensive requirements; hacking/tripping increases possible methods of stopping carriers and injury exposure, potentially deterring certain participants. Removing them alters essential attack and defence actions and participation risk. People disagreeing may rationally refuse to play the other's version, not merely disagree about officials' interpretations.

### C. Fair catch, mark, and free kick at goal: losing a way of turning a catch into a scoring chance

- **Sheffield 1858 Law 3:** a clean catch before ball touches ground yields a free kick; **Law 9 excludes a direct goal from the free kick after a catch**. Sheffield 1862 Law 9 likewise bars obtaining a goal by free kick/catch.
- **FA 1863 Law 8:** a fair catch earns a free kick if the player makes a mark with heel immediately; goal scoring possible from a permitted free kick in the FA code, whereas Sheffield expressly excludes it. (Other free kick situations also existed e.g. touch-down behind goal.) [Sheffield 1858](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Sheffield_Rules_(1858)); [Sheffield 1862](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Sheffield_Rules_(1862)); [FA 1863](https://nationalfootballmuseum.com/items/the-laws-of-the-game-1863/).
- **Sheffield to Nottingham, Sept 1865**: offers offside **in return** for dropping Nottingham's practice of marking the ball and free kick at goal. Undergoing the concession would limit/alter stoppage and scoring opportunities (inference from the provisions); the exact *Notts* code or its original text and whether Notts accepted **not yet verified**. Don't conflate **catch**, **mark**, **free kick** and **direct goal**; they are separate components.
- **Trade-off:** players favouring uninterrupted ground football and fewer free kicks have different preferences from players who value aerial catching, set kicks and direct attempts at goal. However these particular stated motives for Sheffield/Notts are **hypotheses**: the original committee minutes record the *terms*, not why anyone liked or disliked each rule.

### D. Scoring: a surprisingly explicit design motive

- Sheffield introduced **rouges** in its 1862 rules after previously rejecting them (1861), with the early goal **12 feet wide, 9 feet high**. In contemporary coverage of 1862 law changes, the rouge's purpose was expressly to **reduce drawn games**. A goal outweighed any number of rouges; rouges distinguished games tied on goals. Analogy: a lower-tier scoring criterion rather than a modern goal.
- Distinction: These were changes made **within an existing club's preferred code to improve its experience**, not an inevitable compromise demanded by another club.
- Evidence: [Sotheby's catalogue quoting 1862 code](https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2011/english-literature-history-childrens-books-illustrations-l11404/lot.10.html); [Sheffield 1862 written laws](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Sheffield_Rules_(1862)); Sheffield Daily Telegraph **31 Jan 1862, 8 Feb 1862** (secondary citation in the Sheffield Rules reference notes; verify originals).

### Scorecard for evidential strength

| Rule conflict | Direct *rule text* | Stated *incentives/preferences* | Actual *outcome* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sheffield strict vs lenient offside | Yes | Jan 1867 report gives practical dissatisfaction; Sept 1865 minutes give accommodation for Notts | Tried rule, then reverted, corroborated by period accounts (direct original page not inspected) |
| Carrying and hacking, 1863 | Yes | Campbell vs Morley: extensive contemporary speeches | FA adopted ban and Blackheath withdrew; alternative traditions remained |
| Sheffield/Notts offside ↔ mark/free kick at goal | Sheffield and FA provisions yes; Notts exact code not inspected | None beyond conditional proposal | Proposed bargain, **acceptance not confirmed** |
| Sheffield 1862 rouges | Yes | Contemporary newspaper describes prevention of draws | Introduced, later abandoned, no claims about impacts on goal rates |

**Counterintuitive result:** An attractive outsider can induce a compromise, but the new rule may reduce the very *enjoyment/clarity* of football that prompted clubs to organise in the first place. The 1865–66 offside trial shows a club could *reverse* a concession rather than standardise permanently.

**Next stage:** Establish the *fixed, repeated* costs of bilateral rule negotiations (fixtures, communication, officiating, uncertainty) and which individual clubs preferred binding standardisation even when that made their game less enjoyable. That is a separate incentive question, not proof that the FA had to be the solution.

## Incentive 5 — Why agree on a standing set of rules? Why form an association? (19 October 2026)

**Decision question:** Clubs can already play together under match-specific or bilateral codes. When would they prefer durable common laws and a permanent coordinating institution? **Do not assume common rules and association membership are the same decision.** A viable internal club or compatible local match can stop there.

### 1. Explicit player-side benefits before the FA

- **6 Oct 1863, 'Etonensis', _London Daily News_**: Writing about Etonian–Westminster compromise games, said differing codes obstructed matches and prevented players proving skill outside familiar associates. Compromise football degraded skill expression; advocated a single common code. This demonstrates *interoperability* and *comparability of skills*, not merely saving letters or postage. The available digital source is an historical *summary* of the letter, **not its full verbatim text**. https://playupliverpool.com/1863/10/06/lets-frame-rules-for-one-universal-game/
- **1861–62 Dingley Dell, London**: Played games under a local hybrid code; observers writing in _The Field_, 22 Feb/22 Mar 1862, advocated publishing rules and widespread adoption akin to MCC's cricket laws. 'D.D.' letters in _Bell's Life_, Dec 8 1861, proposed principles excluding carrying and hacking and hoped schools would agree. **Author's identity as Dingley Dell's captain George Sills is only conjectural**. These are **pre-FA proposals for standardisation**, showing the FA was not the only possible vehicle. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14660970.2022.2113128 (search index provides relevant excerpts; publisher page may deny open access); https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363745697_A_brief_study_of_football_in_London_from_1800_to_the_founding_of_the_Football_Association_in_1863 . Scholarly source Michael Freeman (2022).

### 2. Documents with unusually explicit motives, Nov–Dec 1863

- **26 Nov 1863: John G. Tupholme, secretary, Louth FC**, replying to Lincoln FC secretary E. A. Chambers. The clubs had played each other previously. Louth had formed a subcommittee to revise rules. Tupholme explicitly wanted Louth and Lincoln to have **rules in common for easier fixture arranging WHETHER OR NOT they joined the London FA**. He personally favoured joining the FA but recognised separate decisions. Read by Morley in FA meeting **1 Dec 1863**, reported in _Bell's Life_ on 12 Dec. **This is the strongest direct recorded case of the transaction-cost argument without conflation with FA membership.** https://playupliverpool.com/1863/12/01/the-fourth-meeting-of-the-football-association/ (the full report, find Tupholme).
- **28 Nov 1863: Edwin H. Ash, Richmond informal footballers.** Declined FA membership as not a continuing club, but expected to adopt its laws for **convenience in matches with other clubs**. This is the **free-adoption/non-member** case, showing a club can use FA's output without joining. Text transcribed from _The Sporting Life_: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Sporting_Life/1863/Football_Association_Meeting and the fuller _Bell's Life_ report above.
- **30 Nov 1863: W. Chesterman, Sheffield FC secretary**: Applied to enrol as association member and thought a general code desirable; simultaneously sent Sheffield's existing code and objected to multiple proposals (no crossbar; ball throwing distance; running/hacking; boot nails). Membership interest was **not** unconditional acceptance of the draft rules. _Bell's Life_ Dec 12: https://playupliverpool.com/1863/12/01/the-fourth-meeting-of-the-football-association/ .
- **10 Nov 1863 FA meeting, Ebenezer C. Morley:** Acknowledged public schools would not immediately abandon local codes, but argued they would recognise advantage of common law when playing association clubs on **neutral ground**. Thought **20–30 clubs** joining would create sufficient authority for the code. A clear **network benefit** argument: the attractiveness of a code depends on how many desirable opponents use it. It was his **expectation**, not a guaranteed historical outcome. _Bell's Life_, Nov 14, reproduced: https://playupliverpool.com/1863/11/10/the-football-association/ .
- **Harrow school captain, Charles Gordon Browne, Oct–Nov 1863:** First requested **specific benefits of joining the FA**, then said Harrow preferred own rules and would watch the experiment from outside. **Explicit counterexample:** common code not worth perceived sacrifice at that point. _Bell's Life_ Nov 14, same link.
- **Charterhouse, B. F. Hartshorne, Oct 29 1863:** declined FA membership 'as yet'; **Westminster** preferred to wait for a separate meeting of **public-school captains**. Suggests *alternative governance / waiting for others*, not consensus or automatic need for London rules. _Bell's Life_ Nov 14.
- **9 Nov 1866, _London Evening Standard_, letter signed 'Hon. Sec., Football Association':** explicitly said objective of developing universal laws was to enable neighbouring clubs to play without rule disagreements; admitted the original split was difficult. Useful **three-years-later statement of organisers' purposes**, not definitive proof why all 1863 founders acted. https://playupliverpool.com/1866/11/09/hacking-at-football-letter-to-the-editor/

### 3. What does a standing association add over two clubs agreeing privately?

**Supported institutional actions, not necessarily uniquely necessary functions:**
- Permanent publication of a *single* rulebook. In 1863 FA founding rules required changes to be advertised in newspapers; FA printed a booklet of laws in Dec 1863. https://www.thefa.com/about-football-association/who-we-are/history
- Proposed membership and subscription: annual **one guinea per club** (FA history). Distinguish club levy from member-level recreational subscriptions (Barnes **one shilling per person**; apples and oranges).
- Regular committee meetings, amendments, correspondence with potential member clubs and schools; reported Nov–Dec 1863.
- A club adopting published common laws without joining association, as **Richmond** expressly envisaged, illustrates that these **services have non-member benefits**.
- But agreement on the **rules** is not agreement on the **best game**, or a reason to give the institution power over your own club's rules. A common code may align *some* matches, not end other codes.

**Hypotheses to test, not established founder-wide motives:** lower recurring bargaining / translation costs, fewer arguments over game legality and officiating, comparable skill ratings across opponents, larger fixture network, convenience of future revisions, predictable participation rules, status/agenda control for founders.

### 4. Key caution against inevitable-FA history

- Rugby-style advocates (Blackheath's F. M. Campbell) resisted dropping running with the ball and hacking, preferred not to join if those were abandoned. Recorded in 1 Dec 1863 debates; different attractive games were at issue.
- Original founding was a **small London group**. Hiroyuki Aonuma (2014) concluded they did **not** intend to enforce rules throughout Britain when founded; examined original meeting documents and later institutional histories: https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jjpehss/59/2/59_13077/_article .
- **FA did not monopolise useful standardisation:** local Sheffield games, Cambridge and Dingley Dell codes, bilateral Louth–Lincoln rule agreements and various Rugby or Australian traditions operated independently or in parallel. The final explanation for **global popularity** is *not* established at this stage.
- **Do not invent** quantitative evidence that negotiating each fixture was expensive; we have contemporary statements about *difficulty and convenience* but not a quantified burden or number of cancelled fixtures caused by negotiation.

### Provisional Incentive 5 finding

There was **documented, variable demand for predictable common rules** amongst people who sought outside opponents. Some consciously considered rule alignment independent of joining the FA (Louth-Lincoln, Richmond); others saw greater advantage as clubs adopted the code (Morley), while schools preferred existing games (Harrow) or waited for rival coordination processes (Westminster). The FA is therefore intelligible as **one proposed continuing solution to an optional coordination challenge**, not the necessary culmination of football.

**Next distinct question**: What did the early association actually do in its first one or two seasons, and did the clubs who joined obtain more games or better quality of play than similar clubs that didn't? Compare memberships/fixtures using *contemporaneous* calendars, published records and correspondence. Don't infer adoption merely from a club appearing in a list. Drafting remains shelved pending incentive analysis.

## Early football beyond Britain: origins, incentives, alternatives (19 October 2026)

**Why examine this:** Up to now, early-club analysis has overrepresented English/Scottish ex-pupils and men organising recreation at home. Overseas early groups show (1) British residents bringing existing games to new places, (2) **local residents taking up a game after seeing it**, (3) the game diverging into different codes, and (4) **problems accessing land and local approval**. This is a targeted corrective to Study 22, **not a new history of worldwide association football or an explanation of how it later dominated**. Chronology matters: many examples are **after** the FA's formation, and are not evidence of its original cause. Distinguish original motives attested by documents from later accounts.

### 1. Melbourne, Australia (1858–1859): football as an optional winter recreation

- Tom Wills's contemporary letter to *Bell's Life in Victoria*, 10 Jul 1858, called for cricket players to organise a football club with laws to keep active in winter and proposed rifle shooting or other athletics as alternatives. In May 1859 members of Melbourne Cricket Club formulated their own code; Melbourne football developed into Australian rules, **not an alternative 'failed soccer'**.
- **Incentive:** off-season activity/fitness (explicit organiser statement). **Choice:** play football, rifle club, other athletic activities. **Result:** distinct football code before 1863 English FA.
- [National Museum of Australia](https://www.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/resources/australian-rules-football); [Victoria Heritage Register, the 1859 Rules, added 2026](https://www.heritage.vic.gov.au/about-us/news/news-stories/footballs-first-rules-now-on-heritage-register).
- Avoid claiming game developed independently of British school precedents; expats/returnees knew British games but they chose their own laws locally.

### 2. Boston, USA (1862–1865): pupils, a locally functioning football game, and late founding myths

- Student Gerrit Smith Miller organised **Oneida Football Club in 1862** among Boston preparatory-school boys; schoolboy football played on Boston Common previously and Oneida had matches against other local school groups. **The exact rules/code cannot safely be labelled modern soccer OR modern American football**; 'Boston game' is associated with local play, which was variously and retrospectively described.
- Club's members mostly left for university in 1865, essentially ending as a playing team. Subsequently former members coordinated and actively promoted their identity as founders of 'American football'; 1925 commemorative monument and later debates about whether this was 'soccer' reveal how history becomes a self-aggrandising origin story.
- **Incentives:** organising more structured contests and friendship (reported by historians); **post-hoc incentive** to claim founding prestige, not evidence initial players set out to invent an American national sport.
- [Boston College news on Marston and Cronin, *Inventing the Boston Game*](https://www.bc.edu/bc-web/sites/bc-news/articles/2025/spring/the-boston-game.html); [Society for American Soccer History by Marston/Cronin](https://www.ussoccerhistory.org/who-were-and-werent-the-oneidas/).

### 3. Lausanne/Geneva, Switzerland (1867 onward): expats want a game, locals discover they like it

- Historian **Philippe Vonnard (2023/2024)** studied primary letters/newspapers from Lake Geneva area, differentiating rugby/association football in first decades. British migrants/visitors included **engineers, merchants, boarding-school teachers and students, holidaymakers**, bringing games from home.
- **21 September 1867**, letter from **Rodney Alfrey** to mayor of Lausanne: 'football society' of c. **20 players** asks permission to play on Montbenon **Monday, Wednesday, Friday** in autumn/winter. **Ground approval**, not coding disputes, was a real barrier, and the group may have played a Rugby-influenced code; club's identity uncertain.
- **20 Jan 1869** *Journal de Genève*: brief announcement of Thursday match between British players from Lausanne/Geneva, showing inter-city games. Rail between cities opened 1858, facilitating travel. Foreign groups already had games.
- **14 Oct 1871** *Journal de Genève*: English residents expressly **welcomed Genevan students** to join games over winter: outreach partly from practical desire for enough players. Not all local students began through compulsion or ex-British-school alumni networks.
- **Edmond Gilliard later recalled** joining football with two friends through a Latin teacher connected to an English family. **Jean Narbel later recalled** seeing British boarding-school boys playing on Saturday walks and thinking 'Why don't we play too?'; helped found **FC Montriond 1896**. These are **recollections**, not 1890s contemporaneous interviews; but direct statements of a motivation underrepresented in English-focused study: **spectator curiosity and imitation**.
- **Late 1880s–1900s** local youngsters formed their own clubs with deeper local roots vs temporary expatriate teams, adopted neighbourhood colours/identities and sometimes wanted year-round football rather than expats' winter recreation. These are **different goals for initiators versus adopters**. Local schools often **resisted** students playing too much rather than requiring participation, opposite of easy assumptions from English schools: Lausanne city council in **1901** complained that football was too exhausting/exciting for school-time and sought to restrict it.
- Practical constraints persisted: landowner of original Servette pitch allegedly expelled players for spoiling his meadow (first annual club report, late 1890s); young players moved repeatedly between grounds due to city building/restricted access. These problems had nothing to do with different offside rules.
- **Core source:** [Philippe Vonnard, 'A tale of clubs, pitches and men', *Sport in History* 2023 (published in 2024 issue), open full text including contemporary archival citations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11340199/). Specifically sections 'Introduction' and 'Finding a home in the city'.
- Beware claims Lausanne Football and Cricket Club in *1860* was definitively association football; contemporary code uncertainty is explicitly emphasised by Vonnard.

### 4. Buenos Aires, Argentina (1867): British migrants form a club and recruit by newspaper

- Historical account by Argentine FA (AFA) says **Thomas and James Hogg** of British immigrant community advertised an open meeting in *The Standard*; formed **Buenos Aires Football Club 9 May 1867** and held a match **20 June 1867** on the **Buenos Aires Cricket Club** grounds. Played **red versus white**, not necessarily two fully independent permanent clubs.
- AFA attributes to Thomas Hogg the belief the game would be an easy and inexpensive recreation for middle- and working-class people, yet records that in practice **British-only elite clubs** initially dominated play. **Ambition to broaden access ≠ actual open participation**. Need original *The Standard* letter before relying on exact words or inclusivity claims.
- **Motives:** expatriate continuity, leisure, club sociability; organiser also professed hope of a wider audience, but evidence for individual native players' motives comes later. Many British railway and school links offered social networks.
- [Argentine FA history (Spanish)](https://www.afa.com.ar/11984/pages/historia) and [2026 AFA article on Thomas Hogg](https://www.afa.com.ar/es/posts/thomas-hogg-un-pionero-en-los-origenes-del-futbol-argentino).
- Note match occurring **4 years after English FA foundation**; not evidence behind 1863 meeting motives.

### 5. Genoa, Italy (1893): sports club founded by foreign residents within later-established international sport

- Genoa's official history preserves a founding record: **7 September 1893**, Genoa Cricket and Athletic Club founded at British consulate by about **30 members paying 10 lire each**, with British expatriate links to commerce and industry. Cricket/athletics and society first; football was subsequently central. This is **after 1863** and an example of club formation by an expat social network with money and other sports, not of the FA origin.
- [Genoa official detailed chronology](https://genoacfc.it/storia-e-museo/), subsection 1893. Avoid anachronistic claim the foundation's name was 'Genoa Cricket and Football Club' in 1893.

### 6. Copenhagen, Denmark (1876–1880s): the game can be added to an existing sport club

- Kjøbenhavns Boldklub (KB) was founded **1876**; per sport historian Andy Mitchell, added association football around **1879**, having begun as multisport. Football was introduced among Danish students through cross-border contacts; James Young Smart, a Scottish footballer who arrived **1885**, later helped translate the laws into Danish in winter **1886–87**. These dates show **institutional import/adaptation** after the original English FA.
- [Club heritage page, FC København](https://www.fck.dk/en/content/history), [Scottish Sport History biographical research](https://www.scottishsporthistory.com/sports-history-news-and-blog/archives/11-2025). More distant comparison; not essential to initial 1863 inquiry.

### Newly discovered early-stage incentives / constraints

| Who? | What do they value / want? | Alternative / impediment | Observed response |
|---|---|---|---|
| British football-playing migrants | Familiar recreation and social network away from Britain | Not enough players locally; no pitch | Invite local students; appeal to mayor or borrow cricket facilities (Lausanne 1867; Buenos Aires 1867) |
| Local young people encountering unfamiliar football | Curiosity, imitation, joining peers in attractive new sport | Don't know rules, lack player network | Watch, learn, be recruited, eventually form enduring local teams (Swiss recollections) |
| Clubs led by expat merchant/engineer group | Cross-border or expatriate identity, recreation among associates | Restricted access or outsiders insufficiently engaged | Club institutions, subscriptions, occasional attempts to recruit; early participation may remain exclusive (Buenos Aires 1867, Genoa 1893) |
| Local government / property owner | Control over public land or protecting private property | Football occupies/ruins meadows; urban land scarce | Permit, prohibit or relocate play; football clubs search for new places (Lausanne 1867, Geneva late 1890s) |
| Youth/club founders competing with existing leisure | Activity attractive for its own sake, not continuation of familiar school game | Other games / school authorities reluctant | Independent local teams and rivalry (Swiss local groups, 1890s) |

**What this changes in Study 22:** The UK-centric 'school alumni reproduce their old football games' explanation **cannot explain all early football uptake**. A game also spreads when outsiders **see it played, want to try it and find participants who teach them**, including where institutions do not sanction or actively discourage the activity. International diaspora and institutions (trade, cricket, railways, boarding schools, migration) can *deliver* a sport, but adoption occurs through locals' own choices. Also, **field access** can be a more immediate and consequential barrier than negotiating rules.

**Do not drift** into later rival codes' relative worldwide success or national governing associations; that is the separate future study. Do not infer every expat group played FA association football; origins of 'football' can refer to rugby or a local code.

## Employer-backed clubs: Newton Heath ↔ Honda FC as a modern works-team comparison (20 October 2026)

### The actual early relationship (Newton Heath, founded 1878)

- **Who formed the club?** Employees in the Lancashire and Yorkshire Railway (LYR) **carriage and wagon department at Newton Heath**, rather than a separately financed entertainment company. Initial matches were against other LYR departments and other railway companies.
- **Who in management supported it?** **Frederick Attock**, the railway's carriage and wagon superintendent appointed in 1877, was central to founding and became the club's first president. His business/political connections provided notable vice-presidents. Evidence comes from David Proudlove, *Work and Play*, historical secondary study, pp. 24–27.
- **What support did LYR give?** The football club used the **works dining room** for business; the railway helped secure its **North Road** ground, near the works, and paid its lease. Club colours green and gold were associated with the railway. Employment opportunities through the railway helped draw players from farther away. **Concrete support**, beyond simply putting LYR in the club's name.
- **What changed?** Newton Heath signed professional players in **1886**, incurred increasing wages and ground-improvement expenditure. In **1891** the club built stands but LYR declined financial support for the project. The relationship ended in **1892**, with the club becoming independent of LYR (and a limited company); the company stopped paying the ground lease. These are later developments, **not evidence about founders' precise mental states in 1878**.
- **Scope:** No demonstrated basis here for claiming the railway legally owned all club assets or that LYR directors pursued a specific modern corporate branding/employee-engagement strategy in 1878. Attock's leadership + financial support + company premises = **employer-backed works team**.
- **Sources:** [Manchester United official history (1878–1909)](https://www.manutd.com/en/club/history/history-by-decade/1878-1909); [David Proudlove, *Work and Play* sample, printed pp. 24–27](https://www.pitchpublishing.co.uk/sites/default/files/associated_downloads/Work%20and%20Play%20SAMPLE.pdf); [Premier League summary of naming and independence in 1892](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4592046/what-is-the-history-behind-every-premier-league-clubs-name).

### Best documented modern analogue: Honda FC, Japan

- **Honda's own corporate site** says the football club was founded as an official **Honda Motor Co. sports club in 1971**, with purposes explicitly including employee activity/engagement and integration with local community. Plays in Japan's national **Japan Football League (JFL)**, representing company rather than merely wearing a sponsor's shirt. [Honda official football team page](https://global.honda/jp/sports/soccer/).
- **Direct journalistic account, 7 June 2023:** *Nikkan Sports* described **most Honda FC players as Honda employees** with ordinary work responsibilities. Captain **Kazuki Matsumoto** worked assembling **marine-engine components** in the morning; team training began at **14:30**. ## Mohun Bagan, Calcutta 1889 — investigation (20 October 2026)

**Editorial purpose:** Assess whether this club introduces a *distinct and evidenced founding mechanism*, rather than appearing because it is famous. **Research notes only; no approved article paragraph yet.**

### Early football and the precise organising problem

- **Before 1889:** Football already had an indigenous scene in colonial Calcutta. British expatriates, soldiers, missionaries and colleges had introduced football (which could refer to different historical codes in earlier decades). Local Bengali youths organised their own teams and clubs, including those associated with Nagendra Prasad Sarbadhikari.
- Local schoolboys and college students played at **Mohun Bagan Villa**, the garden/grounds associated with the wealthy **Mitra family** in North Calcutta. A group there called **Students' Union** is described in later club-supporter histories.
- The better-defined **1889 founding motivation**: groups of players departed the Villa to organise **Aryan** and **Bagbazar** clubs, while the footballers remaining at Mohun Bagan Villa had no corresponding club organisation through which to contest games with these rivals. Three Bengali families, the **Basu/Bose, Mitra and Sen**, supported the formation of **Mohun Bagan Sporting Club, 15 August 1889** at the North Calcutta home of **Bhupendranath Basu/Bose**, its first president (and later president of the Indian National Congress). The **venue and patronage were local Indian/Bengali**, not a club started by expatriate Britons.
- This is a stronger **why form a club?** case than generic 'schoolboys wanted to play': *football already existed at the Villa, but formal organisation, supporters, a ground, and institutional respectability enabled players to organise matches against other clubs.* Beware the claim they *could not* legally play other clubs without a club; that is the account's inference/wording, not a demonstrated universal rule.
- **First reported match after foundation**: vs **Eden Hindu Hostel** students at Villa grounds; described retrospectively, often as 1–0 loss. This was **outside opposition**, not necessarily intra-club games first. First cup: **1893 Cooch Behar Cup**. **The Villa ground was small**; the club subsequently obtained other grounds via patrons.
- **Purpose beyond leisure:** Founders reportedly stressed sporting ability alongside etiquette, schooling, discipline and moral character. Students' membership required parental permission. Scholarly discussion cites the club's **1964 Platinum Jubilee Souvenir** (pp. 1–5) for an organised mission of physical and moral development. This made the institution more than an informal kickabout; it was not, however, automatically a directly anti-British revolutionary organisation in 1889.
- **The 1911 significance is different from founding intention:** Mohun Bagan defeated the East Yorkshire Regiment 2–1 to win the IFA Shield on **29 July 1911**, which acquired enormous symbolic national/anti-colonial importance. This was **22 years after its founding**. Scholars explicitly warn against projecting all the symbolic meanings of 1911 onto the motives of the founders in 1889. Compare Dwaipayan Sen, 'Wiping the Stain Off the Field of Plassey: Mohun Bagan in 1911' (*Soccer & Society*, 2006).
- **No contemporary 1889 founding minute read in this investigation.** Club heritage narratives are retrospective; label personal motives as reported by later sources. The 15 August founding date is **not** evidence of deliberate connection to Independence Day, which started after independence in 1947.

### The boots dispute: tempting, but date caution

- Mohun Bagan supporter accounts say **Dukhiram Majumdar** split from the Villa group because he wanted players to wear **boots** to compete against British opponents, and those who remained preferred barefoot play. Other players left for Bagbazar. This would be a very vivid example of *differences in preferred methods and competitive priorities motivating new clubs*.
- **Chronology conflict:** Supporter narrative dates the schism **1884** and describes Aryan FC forming then; historian Kausik Bandyopadhyay / Boria Majumdar academic treatment, 'From recreation to competition: Early history of Indian football' (*Soccer & Society* 2005), explicitly cites **Aryan founded 1888**. Dukhiram commonly given birth year **1875**; would be about **nine years old in 1884**. The 1884 schism date is questionable. Ronojoy Sen's *Nation at Play* supports Dukhiram's advocacy of boots but **not a verified 1884 schism** in the passage located.
- **Recommendation:** Do **not** insert 1884 as settled fact, nor claim club was founded *solely* over boots without better early documentation. Could mention *a later account describes a boot-wearing disagreement* if truly necessary and sourced transparently. Avoid the sensational version until verified.
- The long-standing **barefoot vs boots** choice had multiple potential causes, including cost and preference, not an assured universal 'indigenous political principle'; historian Ronojoy Sen notes boots cost around Rs 7 and 4 annas per pair in a 1903 advertisement. The 1911 Mohun Bagan side famously featured **ten barefoot players, one booted**.

### Distinctiveness / decision

Potentially **worth one concise paragraph** if the takeaway is: **local players wanted organised external fixtures and Indian patrons used a football club to give youth an institution that fostered fitness, character and competitive standing**. Avoid making it an expansive history of 1911, or treating 1889 as an explicitly planned nationalist demonstration. Compared with existing Study 22 cases, *already-playing group needs recognition/patronage to face other clubs* is distinct enough to consider, although overlapping the general participation/coordination mechanism. No draft prose approved yet.

## Working assessment — fairness, demonstrated skill, enjoyment, accessibility, adoption and spectators (20 October 2026)

**Purpose:** Preserve the discussion's actual conclusions and counterarguments, not retrofit our preferred theory as Victorian motivation. This section updates the arguments in Incentives 4–5; it is **not** an article draft or an instruction to investigate every Victorian player's abilities.

### A. Fairness is not the strongest argument; demonstrating skill is

- **Procedural fairness:** Two clubs agreeing beforehand to play under one code can have a legitimate, fairly administered match, even when one is more familiar with the rules. The rules determine the game being tested. Versatility across codes is itself a possible sporting competence.
- **Comparative fairness:** A result under one code is not necessarily a definitive judgement about two clubs' *general* footballing abilities across codes. Clubs could agree compromises, alternate rules or play return fixtures. Standardisation was **one possible solution**, not an essential prerequisite for enjoyable or legitimate contests.
- **Contemporary motive with direct evidentiary support:** **'Etonensis' (October 1863)** complained that Eton–Westminster compromise matches prevented players using the techniques at which they excelled and impeded recognition of their talent beyond immediate associates. The advantage of a shared game is that players can **develop, exhibit and compare their chosen skills against a wider population of opponents**. This is an argument about demonstration and recognition, **not an explicit claim that the contests were unfair**.
- **Specialisation:** A consistent code may permit deeper development of tactics and specialised abilities; **this is our inference**, not something automatically said by Etonensis. Equally, a footballer may enjoy mastering different codes. Do not caricature versatility as inferior.
- **Source:** Contemporary 'Etonensis' letter reported _London Daily News_ **6 October 1863**, currently accessed by a modern **summary**, not yet full primary transcription: https://playupliverpool.com/1863/10/06/lets-frame-rules-for-one-universal-game/ . The specific quotation beginning 'Etonesis contends that these annoyances...' calls for delegates of Eton, Westminster, Rugby, London clubs and universities to draw up a universal code; check transcription and spelling against a primary image before direct publication. The existence of an explicit contemporary complaint is sufficient for our editorial purposes; **do not initiate an exhaustive comparative skills study**.

### B. What makes a game enjoyable? Preference can resist convergence

- Different handling, hacking, offside, restart and scoring provisions create different **activities and valued skills**, not administrative variants of identical football.
- **F. M. Campbell of Blackheath (FA debates, December 1863)** defended running/hacking as tests of courage; opponents regarded physical severity as a barrier to participation. The debate and Blackheath's withdrawal directly show that some participants preferred a particular kind of game strongly enough to resist a common code. See contemporary meeting accounts linked earlier under Incentive 4: https://playupliverpool.com/1863/12/01/the-fourth-meeting-of-the-football-association/ .
- **Verdict:** Different preferences help explain **resistance to standardisation**, but do not themselves explain why the FA code ultimately spread. Do not assert the most widely adopted code was intrinsically the most enjoyable.

### C. Opponent availability and network effects — plausible mechanism, not automatically the sole cause

- **Poker analogy:** A player may prefer an obscure variant but find opponents for Texas Hold'em. Club football could likewise create a trade-off between preserving one's exact preferred code and finding more opponents. A widely played code becomes attractive in part because **other people already play it**.
- Contemporary material in **Incentive 5** supports a real coordination problem: Louth/Lincoln wanted compatible rules independently of FA membership (Tupholme, November 1863); Richmond could adopt FA laws without becoming a member; Morley expected sufficient sign-ups to lend a shared code authority. Those cases provide firmer evidence than merely applying modern economic terminology.
- **Network effects** describe a plausible amplification process; they do not independently explain *which* code reached critical mass or whether particular clubs switched for that reason. **Promotion, competition entry, peer networks, preferences, geography and negotiated rule changes** are possible complementary explanations. Identify contemporary club decisions rather than claiming one grand cause.

### D. Ease of learning and playing — documented design criterion, causal effect unproven

- **Poker analogy:** Texas Hold'em is generally easier to introduce to a novice than five-card pot-limit Omaha, even though both can be played at a high level. Separate **ease of understanding the rules** from **ease of mastering the game**.
- **John Charles Thring's** article 'Football, Simple and Universal' (_The Field_, **28 December 1861**) and his code _The Simplest Game_ indicate that **simplicity was expressly valued in rule design**. Primary image reference: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Football,_Simple_and_Universal_(The_Field)_1861-12-28.png .
- Contemporary FA consideration of the Cambridge Rules also reportedly praised their simplicity; verify original 1863 meeting wording before quoting. A historical account: https://isfa.org.uk/our-history/the-history-of-isfa/ .
- **Verdict:** Simplicity was genuinely considered in drafting/choosing a code. We have **not established** that association football spread *because* beginners found its rules easier than alternatives. Simplicity could also make administration or agreement easier, not just instruction.

### E. Promotion, organisations and competitions — do not confuse sequence with causation

- The FA's early difficulties, the existing Sheffield football community, the establishment of the FA Cup, Sheffield–FA negotiation and later convergence make a simple 'the easiest/best code naturally won' narrative implausible. These are subjects for evidence-led consideration, not a preselected institutional-victory story.
- **Keep chronology and agency separate:** formation of the FA (1863), spread of its laws, establishment of competitions (FA Cup from 1871), Sheffield code convergence (1877), and rugby/association divergence are related but **distinct explanatory questions**.
- **Provisional interpretation:** Competitions, deliberate promotion and access to opponents may have made the FA code attractive; **relative causal weight still unestablished**. Where possible use club minutes, letters and contemporary publicity to check decisions; later historians help locate and contextualise records, but their explanatory frameworks are not nineteenth-century testimony.

### F. Spectators — an additional possible beneficiary, not an established 1863 motive

- A person familiar with one football code might struggle to interpret tactics, infringements and scoring under another. Common rules could make it easier to follow multiple clubs and understand what counts as a good play. A shared audience may in turn reinforce a shared code.
- Conversely, **novelty of different codes could be entertaining**, and early football clubs did not necessarily organise themselves primarily for paying spectators.
- **Verdict:** This is a defensible **inference** to keep on the board. We do **not yet have contemporary evidence** that spectator comprehension significantly motivated the FA's 1863 standardisation decisions, and should not suggest otherwise.

### G. Modern illustration of different codes — useful, but only an analogy

- **Clash of the Codes, Wigan vs Bath (1996):** Wigan won under rugby league rules **82–6**; Bath won under rugby union rules **44–19**. This illustrates that success depends partly on which version of a game is being played, while both sides can knowingly consent to the conditions. Modern report: https://www.superleague.co.uk/article/1392/hemmings-way-cross-coders .
- These were high-level late twentieth-century rugby sides, **not a valid quantitative proxy for 1860s amateurs**. Training, specialisation, professionalism and game differences make direct comparison unsafe. Neither the scorelines nor the analogy establish why Victorian clubs acted as they did.
- **2015 proposed Leicester Tigers vs American football converts event was cancelled**; it is not evidence of an actually played crossover. Do not combine it with Wigan–Bath.
- **Editorial decision:** A brief crossover illustration may help a reader understand procedural vs comparative fairness, but is optional. Don't turn it into another research project.

### H. Source hierarchy and agreed investigation discipline

1. **Contemporary statement:** What a specific player, club officer, newspaper or meeting actually argued, with the author's interests and exact date identified. Original text/scan where possible; label later transcriptions and abstracts.
2. **Observed conduct:** What a club or association agreed, changed, refused, advertised or entered. These behaviours can support **reasonable inferred motivations** even without every participant leaving testimony.
3. **Historical synthesis / our inference:** Later historians and concepts like network effects are valuable hypotheses and interpretations, but their publication date and analytical status must not be mistaken for contemporary participant testimony.
4. **Editorial sufficiency:** We are **not** undertaking exhaustive club-by-club psychology or Victorian athletic comparisons. Use the strongest conclusion the evidence supports, test plausible alternatives and stop when an argument has been satisfactorily assessed.

**Decisions reached:** Do **not** label 'competitive fairness' a demonstrated principal driver. Retain **demonstrating and recognising skill** as a documented contemporary reason for wanting common rules; **enjoyment and code preference** as documented reasons for disagreement; **simplicity** as a documented design criterion but unproven winner-determiner; **opponent networks, promotion and competitions** as plausible, testable adoption mechanisms; and **spectator understanding** as an additional, currently unverified consideration. Do not impose a predetermined explanation or manufacture historical agreement.

## Incentive 5 addendum — ex-public-school networks, status, and legitimate authority (20 October 2026)

**New gap spotted:** Earlier investigation gave Sheffield's local clubs detailed attention but under-explored former public-school pupils as players, organisers, journalists, university students, club members and subsequent FA officers. Ask how elite school **identity, prestige, connections and football traditions** influenced who felt entitled to recommend rules, rather than saying 'Eton invented football' or reducing events to Sheffield vs London.

### First-hand contemporary indications of status and competing legitimacy

- **Nov 1863 Oxford undergraduate letter to _Sporting Life_, quoted by Graham Curry from Geoffrey Green's earlier citation:** London's clubs lacked sufficient influence, the undergraduate argued; the universities should draw up a code and send it to the London committee, asking whether Barnes, Blackheath etc. should 'dictate' to Eton/Harrow/Winchester. **This is one participant's argument about authority and prestige**, not proof that Oxford/Cambridge controlled the FA or that everybody believed this. Source locator: Graham Curry, _Association Football_ (textual search snippet), https://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/36949/1/GrahamCurry.pdf ; retrieve the actual 1863 letter before direct quoting beyond the verified fragment.
- **1858–59 _Bell's Life_ public-school letters:** 'Juvenis' wanted general laws; respondents identifying with Rugby ('Floreat Rugbœa'), Eton ('Floreat Etona'), Winchester ('Old Wykehamist'), and Marlborough argued in favour of their own traditions and against wholesale adoption of another school's code. The editor reportedly stopped publishing abusive replies. Secondary collation of **dated contemporaneous correspondence:** Michael Freeman, 'A brief study of football in London from 1800 to the founding of the Football Association in 1863' (2022), https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14660970.2022.2113128 and FIFA Museum's discussion https://www.fifamuseum.com/fr/explore/fifamuseumplus/blog/The-quest-for-a-univ . **Inference:** pride, tradition and claimed standing could complicate rule agreement; **not proven:** every school was primarily defending social prestige rather than playing preferences.
- **FA meeting, 10 Nov 1863 (reported _Bell's Life_, 14 Nov):** Morley had written to Rugby, Eton, Winchester, Harrow, Westminster and Charterhouse. Harrow captain Charles Gordon Browne asked what benefit membership offered, then declined and stated affection for existing rules; Charterhouse captain declined to commit; Westminster's W. W. C. Lane said he would wait for the proposed **meeting of public-school captains**. Contemporary meeting report transcription: https://playupliverpool.com/1863/11/10/the-football-association/ . This is stronger than guessing how schools reacted: **some refused, one explicitly deferred to another proposed coordinating forum**.
- **Cambridge (1840s–1863):** Alumni of different schools had to negotiate laws for playing together at university. Cambridge University Library holds **the 1863 rules**; its account notes earlier 1840s attempts are partly reconstructed from **1897 reminiscences**, not surviving first-hand contemporaneous code. https://specialcollections-blog.lib.cam.ac.uk/?p=5891 . See also Eton school archive exhibition catalogue for the perspectives and memoir references: https://collections.etoncollege.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2025/01/Sport_Eton_Catalogue.pdf .

### Who actually founded the FA? Avoid an elite-school takeover story

- **Morley** (Barnes), a solicitor, was *not* a major public-school old boy; his club included alumni who disagreed about playing style. The FA's own retrospective institutional history confirms this: https://www.thefa.com/about-football-association/who-we-are/history .
- Historians **Adrian Harvey** and **Hiroyuki Aonuma** dispute the old story that major public-school old boys dominated the **fourteen representatives of eleven clubs** at the inaugural meeting. Harvey's investigation reportedly counts only **about two probable alumni of the seven major public schools** among attendees (check definition and identities before using a precise fraction in publication). Aonuma's academic abstract: https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jjpehss/59/2/59_13077/_article .
- Do not conflate **status of the major Clarendon public schools** with other fee-paying, boarding, grammar or school-linked institutions in the FA. Nor infer that Sheffield's **educated middle-class** founders were working-class outsiders: Creswick was a solicitor educated at Sheffield Collegiate School and Prest a local merchant. Sources: Sheffield Council https://www.sheffield.gov.uk/news/2023/new-book-about-sheffield-fc-founder-sir-nathaniel-creswick-released and background https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14660970.2016.1276245 .

### Continuity after school: old-boy networks and organisation

- **Charles W. Alcock (Old Harrovian)** helped organise Forest/Wanderers; later FA committee member (from 1866), secretary (from 1870), and proposed FA Cup (1871). The FA's retrospective account connects the cup idea with Harrow inter-house sport. **Do not place Charles Alcock at the inaugural FA meeting in 1863**; his brother John Forster Alcock represented Forest (see original meeting lists). Sources: https://www.thefa.com/news/2016/nov/02/history-of-the-fa-cup and 1863 representative report https://playupliverpool.com/1863/11/10/the-football-association/ .
- Wanderers, Oxford University and public-school old-boy groups were prominent in early FA Cup competition. This shows former students' **continued organisational and sporting influence**, not proof that elite school rules won just because of prestige. Source https://isfa.org.uk/our-history/the-history-of-isfa/ .

### Editorial result and limits

**Add an actor to the historical model:** people associated with public schools **after leaving them** could bring a remembered code, personal prestige, a network of schoolfellows and graduates, and practical knowledge of school competitions into clubs and coordinating bodies. These effects can operate without the school itself joining the FA.

**Preserve three separate propositions:** (1) former schoolboys and universities genuinely influenced adult football; (2) some contemporary writers claimed prestige should confer authority to draft laws; (3) it is **not yet demonstrated** that prestige determined which code ultimately spread. Sheffield's alternative organisation is a useful counterweight, not an exclusive explanation. The social authority question is about **who can persuade people to adopt rules**, not simply who formally attends a meeting.

**Next constrained task:** If examining status further, look at the surviving 1858–59 letters and the 1863 Oxford letter in their original context, then assess representative historical outcomes. No exhaustive prosopography of every schoolboy or club required.

### FA Cup follow-up — what made the FA code worth using (23 October 2026)

**Research question:** Did the 1871 FA Challenge Cup create an actual incentive to *play FA rules*, and is there documentary evidence of clubs switching or joining for that reason, rather than a retrospective "Cup caused universal standardisation" myth? ### 1877 negotiation evidence — a concrete consequence and the vote (23 Oct 2026)

- **Feb 1877 Sheffield–Manchester game, sporting cost of code differences:** Manchester AFC travelled to Sheffield and lost **14–0** in a match under Sheffield rules. Contemporary commentary in the *Athletic News* (3 Mar 1877, p. 1) noticed Manchester's misunderstanding of Sheffield's offside system; their captain **Stuart G. Smith** then wrote to *The Field* (published **10 Mar 1877**) urging one uniform code. Do not imply the rule confusion **alone** caused the large defeat. Peer-reviewed analysis *The Emergence of an Association Football Culture in Manchester 1840–1884*: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17460263.2013.873075 (near discussion of notes 127–129).
- **What nearly sank agreement:** Sheffield had favoured a **kick-in from touch**, while the FA insisted on a throw-in at right angles to the touchline; Sheffield had also resisted the FA's stricter offside rule. After years of failed reciprocal suggestions, a **Clydesdale FC (Glasgow) compromise** retained a throw-in but let it travel **in any direction**. Sheffield withdrew its kick-in proposal to support this; the FA's February 1877 meeting initially **rejected** the compromise, prompting public complaints. At an extraordinary FA meeting **17 Apr 1877**, the compromise passed **33–10**, proposed by J. H. Todd of Clydesdale and seconded by W. Peirce Dix of Sheffield FA. Laws 1877 transcript and original press bibliography: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Laws_of_the_Game_(1877); historically cited Sheffield papers on Petra Tabarelli's research: https://nachspielzeiten.de/sheffield-fa-and-london-fa-1877-2/ . Sheffield Association approved the new FA code at an extraordinary meeting **23 Apr 1877**. Sheffield accepted the stricter offside law and a throw-in rather than kick-in; FA agreed to the freer-direction throw-in (not Sheffield's exact original kick-in). This was **compromise but not equal concessions on every feature**.
- **What contemporary Sheffield press said:** *Sheffield and Rotherham Independent*, 28 Apr 1877, framed result as “the FA and Sheffield have amalgamated” rather than unilateral submission. Graham Curry, *Association Football* p. 109 reproduces this in context, pointing out that some later histories inaccurately say simple capitulation: https://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/36949/1/GrahamCurry.pdf .
- **Caution — source confusion:** The otherwise useful detailed modern post at nachspielzeiten.de labels one discussion of April 1877 changes as switch **from throw-in to kick-in**; its own 17 April report and the transcribed 1877 FA law contradict that statement. Follow the actual new law: **throw-in in any direction**, not kick-in. Also resist claiming the 1877 deal immediately solved all *British* football rules differences; Scottish objections to throw-in direction remained until later negotiations.
- **Narrative fit:** Stronger than dry club/committee disagreement: Manchester lost 14–0, reporters noted confusion, Smith pleaded for one code; after FA rejected the compromise it had to reconvene and reverse course; Sheffield then finally agreed. Prose should stay accessible; at most one parenthetical mention of touchline and offside, not technical mechanics; continue one approved paragraph at a time.

Avoid perpetuating “he brought football to Ireland” unqualified.

Delegates to **Manchester, 6 Dec 1882**: Wales **Llewelyn Kenrick and W. S. Owen**, Ireland **John McAlery and John Sinclair** (Scotland Laurie and Wallace; England Marindin and Peirce Dix). ### Why FIFA never simply absorbed IFAB — 1904 to modern voting

**Key point for eventual modern coda (do not shift 1886 narrative into exhaustive FIFA history):** FIFA founded in Paris **1904** after IFAB had regulated Laws since **1886**. FIFA elected to use Laws approved by IFAB; it was not the organisation that owned the established playing code and did not have unilateral authority to change it. The 1905 FIFA statutes recognised each participating country's association's jurisdiction over its national game. IFAB original four had leverage because games required common agreed laws. IFAB's official history: https://theifab.com/background/ .

**1911 challenge explicitly rejected:** Football historian **Alan Tomlinson**, chapter “FIFA: Beginnings, Tensions, Trajectories” in Stefan Rinke/Kay Schiller eds., *The FIFA World Cup 1930–2010* (2014), p **34** (PDF 35 zero-indexed or PDF P34), writes that in **1911 German FIFA delegates proposed that FIFA should ultimately control laws**. The English FIFA president and FA rejected the proposal outright, initially also refusing FIFA even a seat. In **1913**, a compromise admitted two FIFA representatives to the existing board; see original verified academic book page, https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9783835326064_A22423736/preview-9783835326064_A22423736.pdf . This is unusually strong evidence against simplifying to “FIFA never bothered to take over.”
 
**1913–1958 voting:** Four British home FAs 2 votes each (**8** total), FIFA **2** (total 10); **80%** approval needed. Thus all four home associations together could amend laws without FIFA consent. IFAB 1913 special meeting Wrexham invited FIFA; early 1913 reports collated https://englandsoldestfootballclubs.com/the-international-football-conference-of-1882-the-story-of-the-short-lived-national-football-association-and-the-creation-of-the-international-football-association-board-ifab/ . **FIFA remained on IFAB even when home associations withdrew from FIFA over political/amateurism disputes (1918/1920 and 1928–1946).** FA's own history explicitly calls the FIFA representatives on IFAB “an important channel ... for future negotiation” https://www.thefa.com/about-football-association/who-we-are/history ; different accounts debate wartime representation, so avoid pretending there was uninterrupted FIFA participation 1920–1923.

**1958 renegotiated compromise:** Four British FAs reduced to **one vote each**, FIFA increased to **four** votes (total eight), change needs **six** votes. Both British bloc and FIFA have effective veto: FIFA must recruit 2 of 4 home associations, British bloc must secure FIFA's 4. Recognised 1958 change by detailed history https://thefsa.org.uk/news/mad-experiments-ignoring-fans-a-history-of-football-law-makers/ and historian https://www.thescotsfootballhistoriansgroup.org/the-reckoning ; current official IFAB rules https://www.theifab.com/organisation/ .

**Current institutional arrangement**: FIFA's own statutes explicitly say “Only The IFAB may lay down and alter the Laws of the Game,” https://img.fifa.com/image/upload/qcpshqziazmnmikh6tk5.pdf section 6. IFAB became **legally autonomous Swiss association 2014**, IFAB official https://theifab.com/background/ and https://www.thefa.com/news/2014/jan/13/ifab-reforms-approved-130114 .

**Inference NOT documentary fact:** FIFA could hypothetically attempt to repudiate the existing arrangement, amend statutes and ask all its associations to play by a new FIFA code. But that would depend on member acceptance and risk incompatible codes across jurisdictions; not evidence of a particular FIFA official's private thoughts. The governing body does not gain authority over a separate rule-making body simply through growth. This illustrates article thesis of *authority created through agreements* but belongs only as short modern coda after 1886, not a second 20th-century historical feature. ### Why England missed the Liverpool conference, 23 February 1886 — primary press

**New primary evidence, read rather than inferred**: The *Liverpool Daily Post*, 24 February 1886, describes the previous day's meeting at the North-Western Hotel, Liverpool: “England, who would not agree to the agenda paper put forward, were not represented”, but Scotland, Ireland and Wales attended, discussed privately and approved unanimous proposals to send to the FA. Transcription of actual newspaper report https://www.bluecorrespondent.co.uk/september-april1885-86.html (around “INTERNATIONAL FOOTBALL CONFERENCE”, 24 Feb 1886); original *South Wales Echo* 24 Feb 1886 reproduced as image at https://englandsoldestfootballclubs.com/the-international-football-conference-of-1882-the-story-of-the-short-lived-national-football-association-and-the-creation-of-the-international-football-association-board-ifab/ (linked newspaper crop).

**Agenda** from Queen's Park club historian Richard Robinson 1917, ch. XXVII, https://www.electricscotland.com/history/sport/football/chapter27.htm : (1) define professional, (2) whether every national FA must legalise professionalism, (3) whether amateur clubs may play professionals from another country, (4) provisional arrangement to preserve international fixtures, and (5) rotating annual joint conferences to decide laws, status and other matters. Irish FA proposed the conference. Date 23 Feb 1886, earlier fixed 16 Feb. Newspaper resolutions include a **three-year residence qualification** on professionals from another country, birthplace qualification for internationals, four-FA rotating annual meetings (England to host June 1886), and each FA to decide its own definition of professional.

**Likely background, but not proved as a unique cause**: English FA legalised professionals July 1885. SFA restricted matches with professionals, and three neighbours pressed England about cross-border transfer, team eligibility and player status. The newspaper clearly says England rejected the *agenda*, but **does not specify exactly which item England disputed, whether it objected to others reopening professionalism decisions, or private deliberations**. Avoid asserting a single reconstructed motive.

**Editorial distinction:** professionalism/transfer/selection rules are **association governance or player eligibility**, not the *Laws of the Game* administered by IFAB. The February row shows persistent disagreement among associations and their recognition of need for a standing forum, but don't imply IFAB was established simply to rule on professionalism, wages, or player nationality. Revised proposed para can say: “England stayed away because it would not agree to the proposed agenda, which dealt largely with professionalism and international eligibility”; avoid unexplained absence or undue excursus into professional history.

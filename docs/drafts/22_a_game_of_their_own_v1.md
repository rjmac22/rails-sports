# A Game of Their Own

*Study 22 — first narrative draft. Working title and text, not yet subedited or pitched.*

In Sheffield, one way to find an opposing football team was to divide the players alphabetically.

Sheffield Football Club had plenty of members. By the end of its first season, in 1858, around sixty-two names appeared in its records. What it did not have was a regular supply of other football clubs to play. So its members played one another.

Half a century later, John Charles Shaw, an early member, remembered dividing the club alphabetically to make the games interesting. Other early contests pitted married men against unmarried men. There was no requirement that one permanent team should always face another permanent team. The people who turned up could make their own sides.

The club was already doing what its members wanted it to do: providing opportunities to play football.

It is easy to look back at a club founded in 1857 and see the beginning of the organised sport that followed. But that reads the end of the story into its beginning. Sheffield's members did not have to build a league, attract a crowd or agree with clubs hundreds of miles away about the correct way to play. They first needed some men, a ball, a suitable place and a game they wanted to come back for.

## Getting a game

Forming a club wasn't the only way to play. Friends could agree to meet, former pupils could return to a familiar game, or an existing cricket club could make room for football. A club was useful when people wanted the arrangements to last.

The surviving accounts of Sheffield show what that meant in practice. Members paid subscriptions. Officers kept minutes. The club bought six footballs for £2 6s, organised matches among its own members and even ran an annual athletics day. In October 1858, its committee debated both the rules governing membership and the laws governing play.

Those were different questions. One concerned who could join, who collected the money and who made decisions. The other concerned what players could do with the ball and with one another.

Neither problem was new.

In Edinburgh, John Hope's football club had kept subscriber lists as early as 1824. Receipts survive for hiring Dalry Park, where the members also practised archery, and for the work of inflating footballs. Whatever precise version of football the members played, their paperwork records some of the practical necessities of keeping a sporting group going.

At Barnes, in southwest London, an 1862 rulebook made the arrangement unusually clear. A member paid one shilling a year. The club's balls and other property were stored at the White Hart public house. Any four members could obtain them for use. Members' friends and local residents could take part, provided the captain did not object.

That wasn't a fixed professional eleven waiting for its next fixture. It was a way of making football available to a group of people.

Not everybody even wanted a permanent club. In November 1863, Edwin Ash explained that his football-playing friends in Richmond were mostly young men preparing for the army. They would soon move away. There was little point establishing an institution that might not survive the winter. They could simply get together for a few matches.

The choices were familiar: organise something enduring, borrow an existing arrangement, or just play when enough people were available. The appropriate answer depended on what the participants wanted.

## Which game?

Agreeing to play football didn't settle how to play it.

Schools and local groups had developed different versions. Some permitted running with the ball; some emphasised kicking and dribbling. The permissible contact between opponents varied. So did the treatment of a fair catch, the way a goal was scored and the position a player could occupy ahead of the ball.

Sheffield's members didn't wait for somebody else to decide. They discussed laws, amended them and printed their own. In 1858 they permitted a fair catch but forbade carrying the ball along the ground. Hacking and tripping were prohibited. Their later rules included a narrower goal and a secondary score, the *rouge*, which offered a way of distinguishing matches that would otherwise end level on goals.

These choices made a particular kind of game possible. Changing the rules could change the skills it rewarded or the risks players faced. The question wasn't whether Sheffield had discovered the one correct version of football. It was whether its members had a version they wanted to play.

Nor did a football game have to develop along the same lines elsewhere.

In Melbourne in July 1858, the cricketer Tom Wills wrote to a newspaper suggesting that local cricketers form a football club to keep active during the winter. He thought football might improve their fitness and even help firm up the cricket ground. If football wasn't practical, he suggested a rifle club or other athletic games instead.

For Wills, the problem was winter inactivity. Football was one possible answer.

A different set of rules was subsequently developed in Melbourne. It became part of the tradition now known as Australian rules football. The point isn't that Melbourne's footballers had failed to agree with distant players. They had organised a game for their own purposes.

## Wanting somebody new to play

A club with enough members could stop there. Sheffield's games were not incomplete because its opponents came from within its own membership.

But familiarity had a cost. However teams were divided, players faced men whose abilities and habits they knew.

Shaw later recalled helping establish Hallam Football Club in 1860 for the purpose of providing opponents for Sheffield. He persuaded Sheffield members to join the new club. When the teams met on Boxing Day that year, they had a new rivalry without needing to replace the local game they already understood.

Shaw wrote about this in 1907, nearly fifty years after the event, so his recollection isn't the same as a founding-minute explanation from 1860. But the reason he gave was strikingly ordinary: he wanted more interesting matches.

The same desire surfaced in Glasgow.

Queen's Park Football Club established itself in 1867 with an explicit purpose recorded in its constitution: the recreation and amusement of its members. Its members played amongst themselves, organised sides and agreed playing rules. They could have continued that way.

By April 1869, however, the club had played only one match against an outside opponent. The committee discussed seeking more fixtures to increase the vigour of its own football. Enquiries went out to other towns.

There were limits. Queen's Park had already declined a proposed trip to Ayr in 1868. Railway travel would cost the players money, convenient trains were difficult to arrange and holidays made it harder to assemble enough men. A nearer club, Thistle, was much easier to play.

Outside opponents were desirable, not desirable at any price.

And internal matches never ceased to be an alternative. When outside fixtures remained scarce in 1872, Queen's Park arranged a series between the president's team and the captain's team. The purpose was to sustain interest and develop the players' abilities.

The members wanted a challenge. They had more than one way of finding it.

## What shall we play?

Playing another club brought a fresh difficulty. Both sides might enjoy football, yet disagree about what the game allowed.

The answer was sometimes to negotiate.

In September 1865, Sheffield's committee recorded a proposed bargain with Nottingham. Sheffield would accept an offside restriction if Nottingham gave up provisions involving marking a free kick and taking free kicks at goal.

That was no trivial exchange. An offside rule affected where attackers could stand and how teammates could advance the ball to them. Fair catches and free kicks created different ways to stop play or threaten goal. Agreeing a fixture could mean surrendering actions that had been legal in your usual game.

The record establishes Sheffield's proposal, not whether Nottingham accepted those precise terms.

Other arrangements were more flexible. Clubs sometimes used one side's rules for an away fixture and the other's for the return. They agreed the number of players, the duration and the treatment of particular actions. Queen's Park's surviving correspondence with Thistle in 1868 discusses a twenty-a-side game, a proposed two-hour duration and changing ends so neither side kept an advantage from the wind and ground.

Sometimes a club was prepared to go further. In 1869, Queen's Park offered to meet the rugby-playing Glasgow Academicals under Rugby rules. When the Academicals declined, Queen's Park members debated adopting Rugby laws alongside their existing code so that such matches might become possible. A majority approved the idea. No resulting Rugby fixture is recorded.

Sometimes neither side would give way.

In June 1871, Queen's Park accepted a challenge from Airdrie and sent a copy of its rules. Airdrie discovered that handling the ball was prohibited. It asked Queen's Park to delete the restriction. Queen's Park refused, and the fixture was abandoned.

The disagreement did not end the relationship. The two clubs had met before and played again in 1872. They simply couldn't agree on that particular game.

What made a compromise worthwhile? An unfamiliar opponent, a better test of ability, a chance to represent your club. What might make it unacceptable? The loss of actions you enjoyed, risks you didn't want to take, a long journey or a game you no longer recognised as your own.

Different clubs could reasonably make different choices. The same club could do so on different occasions.

## A club without a parent

The distinction between enjoying a game and joining an organisation remained important as football travelled.

In Turin in 1897, a group of Italian schoolboys who had taken to the imported game established their own club. They called it Juventus, a reference to their youth. Their early team wore pink. They didn't enter the Italian national championship until 1900.

Six years after its foundation, the club adopted black-and-white striped shirts associated with Notts County in England. It borrowed a sporting identity from abroad, just as its founders had taken up a sport they hadn't invented.

But Notts County did not found Juventus. Nor did the boys need an established football club in Britain to grant them permission to enjoy the game.

The useful question is not why these Italian boys joined a story that had started elsewhere. It is why they wanted to play football at all. According to Juventus's later club history, they were friends who enjoyed a newly imported game and wanted a club of their own.

That was enough to begin.

## One more choice

There was nevertheless an advantage to not having to negotiate a fresh game every time unfamiliar clubs met.

On 26 November 1863, John Tupholme of Louth Football Club wrote to Lincoln. Their clubs had already played one another, and Louth was considering revising its rules. Tupholme wanted them to agree on common playing laws because it would make arranging future matches easier. He thought this desirable whether or not either club joined the new Football Association being organised in London.

His distinction matters.

Two clubs could agree to play by the same laws without joining anything. A group could take a published rulebook and use it for convenience. An association offered another possibility: a continuing set of laws that more clubs might adopt, reducing the need to negotiate separately with each opponent.

In October 1863, a handful of London clubs and representatives began forming the Football Association. Its members disagreed over what the new laws should permit, notably running with the ball and hacking opponents. Blackheath's Francis Maule Campbell defended aspects of the more physical game and resisted abandoning them. Others believed those risks would discourage adults from taking part. A common code did not mean common preferences.

An association was one solution to a coordination problem, and a solution only some footballers needed or wanted. Sheffield had already been playing. Clubs in Scotland negotiated, adapted or refused fixtures according to their circumstances. Footballers in Melbourne were developing another game. Long afterwards, boys in Turin founded Juventus because they wanted to play.

The first question was never who should govern football.

It was what kind of football people wanted to play, and with whom.

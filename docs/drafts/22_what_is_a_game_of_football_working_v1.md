# What is a game of football?

*Study 22 — working first draft, 21 October 2026. For collaborative revision; not fact-checked for publication or subedited.*

People had been playing football in Britain for centuries. Different communities had their own traditions and ways of organising games, and there was no single set of rules governing how football should be played.

The game belonged to the people who played it. A village could arrange a match as part of a celebration. Schoolboys could play among themselves. A group of adults might agree to meet regularly. They did not need another town, a national association or even a permanent club in order to enjoy their football.

But the way people organised to play mattered. It determined who could take part, when they met, which games they knew and whom they might eventually play.

## Football at school

By the middle of the nineteenth century, England's leading public schools had established football traditions of their own. Rugby, Eton, Harrow and others had games that pupils learned over years of playing together. Some schools had also written down their laws. Rugby did so in 1845; Eton's surviving written rules for the Field Game date from 1847.

The schools had not invented the idea of football or gained the right to tell everyone else how to play it. Their importance was different. They possessed established games, recognisable traditions and the considerable prestige of institutions educating many of Britain's influential families. Their former pupils also had reason to continue playing the football they knew.

That influence extended beyond the school gates. At Cambridge University, students arrived with different ideas of football, often shaped by the schools they had attended. They wanted to play together, but they had not necessarily learned the same game.

They could choose one school's laws and ask everyone else to adjust. Or they could devise something acceptable to more of them. Cambridge students made several attempts at common rules; a surviving set from 1856 bears the names of men associated with Eton, Rugby, Harrow and Shrewsbury.

This was already a negotiation between footballers with different preferences. Written laws made an agreement possible, but did not remove the need to decide which game people actually wanted to play.

## A club of their own

Football clubs were not invented in Sheffield in 1857, though Sheffield FC is now celebrated as the oldest surviving one.

In Edinburgh, a young law student named John Hope organised a Foot-Ball Club in 1824. Its members paid subscriptions, hired grounds, acquired equipment and arranged their attendance. The surviving accounts show football being run as a recurring activity, not just a match somebody happened to suggest. The club continued into the 1840s and then disappeared from the record.

London provides another example. In 1849, Mr Denison proposed reviving football at the Oval, using an existing group of cricket clubs and their ground. He recalled an earlier society that had played football and wrestling, but whose football had declined after its available grounds became too small. The new Surrey Football Club would use the Oval during the cricket off-season, with scheduled afternoons and subscriptions to cover its costs.

Neither Edinburgh nor Surrey needed the Football Association; it did not exist. Their organisers needed willing players, a suitable place, equipment and some means of arranging for those things to come together repeatedly.

There were practical reasons to create a club, and equally practical reasons not to. Friends could arrange their own games. A school or cricket club might already supply the people and ground. A temporary group might have no reason to establish an institution that would outlast its members.

Clubs were also part of a broader nineteenth-century expansion of voluntary associations. More people were organising social and recreational activities through societies with members, officers and subscriptions. Football fitted an existing pattern. It was not suddenly made possible by the invention of leisure or by a new desire to kick a ball.

That matters when we turn to Sheffield. We know so much about its club partly because it survived, remained influential and left unusually detailed records. Earlier clubs came and went. A history told only through clubs that later mattered to association football risks making its surviving predecessors look like its inventors.

## Making a game happen

Sheffield FC was founded in 1857 by Nathaniel Creswick and William Prest. Its early members included businessmen, professionals and men connected with local schools and other sports. They organised football in much the same spirit as many other recreational clubs: finding time to meet, pooling money and agreeing how their activity would be run.

By the end of its first season, the club had about sixty-two members. They paid subscriptions and bought footballs. Its officers kept minutes, and its members held athletic competitions as well as playing football.

There was no need for a fixed team of eleven men to face another club every week. Sheffield's members could divide into teams from within their own membership. An early participant, John Charles Shaw, later recalled games between sides selected alphabetically. Other matches divided married men from unmarried men.

For these men the club was already useful. It gave them an organised opportunity to play. It didn't need to become part of a national competition to justify its existence.

But playing the same acquaintances presented a limitation. Once you knew their abilities and habits, an unfamiliar opponent offered a different test.

Shaw later recalled helping establish Hallam FC in 1860 specifically to provide Sheffield with another team to play. His account was written nearly fifty years later and must be treated as a recollection, not as a record of everyone's founding intentions. Yet the result is clear: Sheffield and Hallam met that Boxing Day, and local football increasingly involved matches between separate clubs.

Others followed. By 1862, records identify eleven teams active around Sheffield, most of them playing under the rules Sheffield FC had developed. The local football network existed before the Football Association met in London.

More clubs meant more possible opponents. That could make belonging to a club more rewarding and starting another one more attractive. Sheffield and Hallam illustrate how demand for a new challenge might encourage another organisation into existence.

It was not a universal formula. Access to grounds, personal connections, time and money still mattered, and different towns developed at different speeds. But the local growth did not require anyone to set out to establish a national sport. It could begin with people who simply wanted somebody else to play.

## The price of a new opponent

The attraction of playing another club came with a difficulty: the other club might not play the same football.

That was not just a dispute over obscure clauses in a rulebook. Different customs changed what players were allowed to do, which abilities mattered and which risks they accepted. A team could agree to a perfectly fair contest under unfamiliar conditions and still feel unable to demonstrate the skills it had spent years developing.

The public schools offered a warning as well as a model. Their games were respected and their rules were available, but their former pupils did not agree on which school's football everyone should use. At Cambridge, compromise had been necessary precisely because different traditions were valued.

Independent clubs faced similar choices. They could play under the host's rules, negotiate particular conditions, agree a different code for a return fixture, or decline to play at all. An opponent might be interesting, but not interesting enough to abandon the game you enjoyed.

Some clubs were prepared to play more than one version of football. Sheffield's later records show that its members could accept a rule used by outside opponents while retaining their accustomed arrangements for matches with local clubs. Agreement for one fixture did not oblige them to remake every game they played.

The benefit was a wider choice of opponents. The cost could be the loss of familiar techniques, additional inconvenience or a contest the players no longer particularly wanted. Different groups would place different values on that exchange.

## Making agreement easier

When clubs dealt with the same opponents regularly, they could settle these questions between themselves. But the attraction of a wider playing network also created an incentive to avoid having to negotiate each match from the beginning.

On 26 November 1863, John Tupholme, secretary of Louth Football Club, wrote to Lincoln FC. The clubs had played one another. Louth was considering revising its laws, and Tupholme wanted them to have rules in common so arranging future matches would be easier.

Crucially, he thought that worthwhile whether or not either club joined the Football Association then being established in London.

His interest was the opportunity to play other people without settling the game afresh every time. An association might help achieve that, but it was not the only way. Two neighbouring clubs could make their own agreement.

Tupholme also wanted to know whether Eton, Harrow and Rugby were joining the new association. Those schools were, in his words, 'looked up to' for their practice of football, and their support would influence how others regarded the project. Social standing made a school's rules worth considering. It could not force independent clubs to adopt them.

The Football Association had been formed in October 1863 by London-area football representatives. Its members were trying to establish laws that more clubs could use. Yet they brought their own preferences into the room, just as Cambridge students and local clubs had done.

In December, the disagreement became visible. Blackheath's Francis Maule Campbell defended a more physical form of football, including running with the ball and hacking opponents. Others opposed the practice and the risks it imposed. The association chose laws without those provisions. Blackheath withdrew rather than accept that version of the game.

Meanwhile, Sheffield had already built up years of football under its own laws. Its secretary wrote to the FA in November 1863 welcoming the attempt to establish common rules while objecting to parts of the proposed code. A desire to find agreement did not mean accepting someone else's judgement of what football ought to be.

And joining an association did not automatically settle matters. The Sheffield and London versions continued alongside each other, influencing and challenging one another for years. The Sheffield association eventually accepted a unified code in 1877. That later outcome should not obscure the choices available to Sheffield's players in 1863.

## The game they chose

There was no meeting at which Britain's footballers collectively decided to invent modern football. There were people enjoying established games, schools preserving their traditions, adults organising recreation, clubs seeking opponents and players deciding how much they were willing to compromise.

Some found an agreement worth making. Others preferred the game they already had.

The creation of associations and the spread of common laws changed what was possible. They gave clubs access to more opponents under familiar conditions. The wider the network became, the more valuable participation in it could be. But that advantage was not the same as proving any one version of football was objectively the best.

The Football Association was one consequence of these decisions, not their original purpose. Footballers had been making arrangements to play long before it existed, and those arrangements did not cease simply because a London association published its laws.

The question for each group had been much more immediate: **what sort of game do we want to play, and who will play it with us?**

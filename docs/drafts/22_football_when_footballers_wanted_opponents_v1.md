# When Footballers Wanted Someone Else to Play
**Rails Sports — Study 22 | Working draft v1 | 19 October 2026**

*Status: Early explanatory feature draft, not publication-ready. Scope: why groups organised football and outside games, and why different codes could coexist before and during the FA's formation. Not a claim to explain why association football later spread worldwide.*

---

In 1860, John Charles Shaw helped establish another football club in Sheffield. His own club already had players. It had a place to play and rules for the game. Nothing about the existing arrangement required another organisation.

He wanted an opponent.

Almost fifty years later, Shaw remembered how Sheffield Football Club had divided its members into different sides, sometimes alphabetically, to make the games interesting. He recalled starting Hallam Football Club and persuading some Sheffield players to join it for the purpose of providing opposition to their old club.

On Boxing Day 1860, Sheffield met Hallam at Sandygate. They played according to the Sheffield game. There was no Football Association to give them permission or prescribe a universal code. It would not exist until 1863.

That simple sequence — people find a way to play, enjoy it, then decide they might enjoy challenging somebody else — is missing from many tidy histories of football. Those histories often begin with different rules and end with the organisation that supposedly resolved them. But having different rules was not, by itself, a problem.

## A perfectly good game

At Rugby School, football had rules by 1845. The written regulations went considerably further than deciding what players could do with the ball. They covered announcing matches, attendance and what happened when boys stayed away without a proper excuse. Former pupils could return to play, subject to approval.

The school had players, grounds, opportunities to organise matches and people empowered to make decisions. Whatever disputes might arise during play, Rugby had a functioning game.

Other schools had their own versions. Eton, Harrow and Winchester were not simply failing to agree on the rules of what would one day be called association football. Their pupils played games suited to their own institutions, spaces and traditions. Some of those games survive.

A pupil who enjoyed one version of football might leave school and want to carry on. But the arrangements that had made playing straightforward were no longer there. He needed people, somewhere to play and a way of organising them.

Different groups solved that problem differently.

Former pupils of Blackheath Proprietary School carried on playing their familiar football. Their club's retrospective history describes how they brought in outsiders when there were too few former pupils to make up a side. Sheffield brought together local professional men who wanted regular recreation. In Edinburgh, John Hope's earlier football club had maintained membership records and paid for facilities and equipment.

None of that required a national governing body. Nor did it require every group to play the same game.

## An opponent changes the attraction

Sheffield's members could play one another. Their early fixtures did not need a league or a permanent rival. They could divide the membership by marital status, surnames or other convenient arrangements.

But an outside club offered something else. Sheffield could now play *Hallam*. A result could be associated with two continuing organisations, rather than two temporary selections of members.

We have unusually direct evidence of at least one participant's motive. In a letter written in 1907, Shaw recalled that arranging internal sides had eventually become less interesting and that he helped form Hallam to create opposition. The memory was recorded nearly half a century after the events, so it deserves caution. Still, it is more informative than the assumption that clubs simply needed someone to resolve their disagreements about the laws.

Outside games also raised questions that internal play did not necessarily settle. Who would represent the club? Would the strongest players be chosen? If some were left out, would they still get opportunities to play? Clubs later developed first and second teams, and sometimes thirds. But we should not project those later arrangements backwards: even in 1860, teams did not necessarily field eleven men or select by the standards of a modern professional side.

More importantly, **wanting to play an outsider did not mean wanting to abandon your own game**.

Two groups familiar with the same code could meet without compromising. If they preferred different games, they could choose one code, alter the rules for a particular match, alternate between versions — or decline the fixture altogether.

There was no obligation to produce a single universal game.

## A different answer in Melbourne

The same broad organisational questions appeared elsewhere, but they did not always produce the same answers.

In July 1858, the cricketer Tom Wills called for a football club in Melbourne to keep cricketers active over winter, with a committee to draw up rules. School sides played football that August. In May 1859, a group in Melbourne wrote ten rules of its own.

That code became the basis for Australian rules football.

Here was a group of footballers, many familiar with British sporting traditions, organising players and agreeing laws without waiting for London's Football Association. They weren't developing a failed or unfinished form of association football. They were establishing a game they could play.

## What the FA did — and didn't — settle

In October 1863, representatives of a small group of London clubs established the Football Association. They did not arrive at the meeting as spokesmen for every footballer.

Nor did agreeing to form an association mean agreeing on the game. The drafts debated that autumn differed substantially from the rules ultimately adopted. Carrying the ball and the practice of hacking an opponent's shins were contentious. Blackheath opposed their removal and withdrew.

The choice made by the remaining members established a code for those prepared to play it. It did not make the other games disappear.

Sheffield continued using its own rules for years. Rugby-style football continued. Melbourne's game went its own way. The schools retained distinctive games.

The central question was never why everybody needed the FA. Everybody didn't.

Some footballers wanted to keep playing the game they knew. Others wanted new opponents, and were prepared to make concessions to get them. Some found sufficient agreement locally; others chose a more permanent association.

**Football wasn't waiting for one authority to finish inventing it. People already had games worth playing. The interesting history is what made them choose to change — or not.**

## Editorial and evidence notes — NOT FOR PUBLICATION

**Core claim already supportable:** Existing football groups could play under local arrangements; where they sought outside opponents, they encountered choices about eligibility, organisation and possibly rules; the FA was one response, not a universal necessity.

**Support by section:**

1. *Sheffield and Hallam:* Sotheby's catalogue of Sheffield FC's original 1858–1860 minute books, match records, 1859 rules and Shaw's 18 Oct 1907 reminiscence. https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2011/english-literature-history-childrens-books-illustrations-l11404/lot.10.html . Shaw's motive is his *retrospective* account, not verified independent testimony from 1860.
2. *Rugby School 1845:* Contemporary printed laws with attendance, match organisation and returning former pupils. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Laws_of_Football_as_played_at_Rugby_School_%281845%29 . Transcription, not physical original inspected.

4. *John Hope Edinburgh, 1824:* Membership records, expenses, grounds. https://blog.nrscotland.gov.uk/2018/04/09/trailblazers-the-worlds-first-football-club-with-john-hutchinson-andy-mitchell/ . **Do not imply their game was association football.**
5. *Melbourne, 1858–1859:* Contemporary Tom Wills letter quoted by the National Museum of Australia; Melbourne's rules and dates. https://www.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/resources/australian-rules-football ; original transcription https://www.australianculture.org/winter-practice-1858/ .
6. *1863 FA deliberations:* Contemporary *The Sporting Life* report (transcription), showing votes on hacking, Cambridge rules, dissenting clubs. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Sporting_Life/1863/Football_Association_Meeting ; Independent Schools Football Association summary https://isfa.org.uk/our-history/the-history-of-isfa/ .
7. *Sheffield continuing beyond 1863:* Sheffield archive describes separate rules, later decisions about offside and negotiation over rules of external matches. Same Sotheby's catalogue; don't overclaim all international spread.

**What must not creep into edits:**
- A universal claim that *all* clubs existed because old schoolboys wanted to keep playing.
- The claim that the FA imposed its rules in 1863 or caused Melbourne's code to emerge.
- The claim that selection of the eleven best players motivated formation of Hallam; not established.
- 'Every club outside the FA was rebelling.' Many had little reason to join, and codes could influence one another.
- Ascribing intentional myth-making to the FA without examining its specific official statements.
- Describing the Sheffield/Hallam line from Shaw as contemporary evidence.
- Claiming this feature explains **how or why the FA code later became globally dominant**. That is still unresearched and would require further evidence.

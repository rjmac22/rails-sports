# What is a game of football?

*Study 22 — wholly new first narrative draft, 21 October 2026. Working title; not subedited or approved. Written from the research findings, not by revising v1. Focus: early football as a collection of established games, the choices made when clubs sought opponents, and the difference between common rules and agreeing on someone else's game.*

On 30 November 1863, Sheffield Football Club wrote to the newly formed Football Association in London. The club wanted to join, and its secretary, William Chesterman, welcomed the attempt to establish a common set of laws.

Then he explained what was wrong with the laws the association was considering.

There ought to be a crossbar, he thought, so the umpire could judge whether a goal had been scored. Sheffield players were accustomed to wearing ordinary nails in their boots, which the proposed laws would prohibit. And two provisions allowing a player to run with the ball while opponents held, tripped or kicked him were, in Chesterman's judgement, "directly opposed to football".

That last objection sounds extraordinary now. How could a football club accuse the Football Association of misunderstanding football?

The answer begins with a fact easily obscured by a century and a half of organised competition: people had been playing football long before anyone established the FA. They did not all mean the same thing by it.

## A game people already knew

Football did not arrive in Sheffield in 1857 as a finished invention brought from London. Nor did its founders have to discover it at Eton or Rugby.

Different kinds of football were played in schools and communities. The prestigious schools had deep traditions, particular techniques and, in several cases, written laws. Rugby published laws in 1845; Eton recorded the rules of its Field Game in the 1840s. Boys learned their school's football and sometimes continued playing it after leaving. These games were familiar enough to be discussed, compared and argued over in the sporting press.

They weren't the only football traditions.

There is evidence of organised football challenges in the Penistone and Thurlstone area, north of Sheffield, in the 1840s. Sheffield Collegiate School also played football. When adults who had experienced different games wanted to play together, they did not necessarily arrive knowing the same rules.

University footballers faced that problem particularly clearly. Students at Cambridge came from schools whose versions of football differed. They made repeated attempts to reconcile them. The surviving Cambridge laws of 1856 and 1863 were products of that practical encounter between existing games, not the invention of football itself.

Sheffield's founders could therefore know how to play football, draw on local experience and consult written laws from established schools without ever having attended the most prestigious of them. Research into the club's first members identifies businessmen and professionals alongside a substantial group of former Sheffield Collegiate pupils. That tells us something about the people organising early club football, but it does not establish that one school supplied their entire game.

The members had a simpler immediate problem: they wanted to play.

## Enough people to play

Sheffield FC began in 1857. Its members organised games among themselves, divided into sides by arrangements that included alphabetical order and married versus unmarried men. They collected subscriptions, bought footballs, appointed officers and established regular playing arrangements.

A football club did not have to be a fixed eleven permanently facing other clubs. It could be a way of getting enough people together for a game.

Nor did everyone need a club to play. At Barnes, an 1862 rulebook made the balls available when four members requested them, and permitted guests and local residents to join subject to the captain's approval. In Richmond in 1863, Edwin Ash's group consisted mostly of young men who expected to move away after preparing for the army. They played a few matches during the winter and saw little point in creating a lasting institution.

Football could be enjoyed with or without a permanent organisation. When the players knew one another and shared the rules, there was no need to ask which national body had authorised their game.

The interesting difficulty emerged when a group wanted to play people who had learnt a different one.

## Somebody else's football

An outside opponent offered something an internal game could not: unfamiliar skills, unfamiliar tactics, and an opportunity to judge your own abilities against people beyond your usual circle.

But meeting another club wasn't always as simple as finding a field and agreeing on a Saturday afternoon.

Could a player run with the ball in his hands? Was a fair catch rewarded with a free kick? Where might an attacker stand relative to the ball? Was tripping permitted? Could somebody score without putting the ball between the goalposts?

Different answers didn't merely alter the paperwork. They changed what the players could attempt, which skills mattered and what the contest demanded of them.

A correspondent calling himself *Etonensis* complained in October 1863 about matches between Eton and Westminster played under compromise rules. His complaint, as reported in the *London Daily News*, was not simply that the arrangements were inconvenient. The compromise prevented players using many of their familiar techniques. Neither side could demonstrate the football at which it had become proficient.

There was a reason to want one common game: it would allow someone to develop a particular set of skills and test them against a wider pool of opponents.

But there were reasons not to want a common game, too—at least not one that removed the parts of football you enjoyed.

Some clubs solved the problem one fixture at a time. They accepted an opponent's laws, offered concessions or proposed using different rules in the return match. A club that usually played without a strict offside law could agree to one elsewhere. It might change the game for that afternoon without renouncing its own rules.

And sometimes an agreement wasn't worth making. No match was better than one played under conditions you found unacceptable.

## One set of rules, but whose?

On 26 November 1863, John Tupholme of Louth Football Club wrote to the secretary of Lincoln FC. The two clubs had played each other. Louth was reviewing its laws, and Tupholme wanted them to agree on rules in common to make further fixtures easier to arrange.

He made an important distinction: this would be useful **whether or not either club joined the new London association**.

He was personally enthusiastic about the FA and hoped Eton, Harrow and Rugby would join. Their reputation gave those schools influence over what others might regard as proper football. But he did not confuse their prestige with a legal power to make everyone play their way.

A shared code would save clubs from settling every disputed provision afresh. The more opponents who used it, the more useful it could become.

Yet even someone who wanted standardisation had to ask: whose code?

Chesterman's letter from Sheffield makes the difficulty unusually plain. Sheffield supported the Association's attempt to establish general laws, wanted to join, and still objected to rules that would alter the nature of its football.

At the Association's meeting on 1 December, the disagreement became sharper. Francis Maule Campbell of Blackheath defended the running game, in which a player could carry the ball and opponents could stop him physically. He regarded the courage it demanded as part of football's appeal.

Ebenezer Cobb Morley, representing Barnes, took a different view. Whatever his own tolerance for rough play, he believed allowing hacking would discourage adult men from taking part. A game whose rules deterred prospective opponents was a poor basis for a wider association.

Neither side was merely correcting a typographical error. They were deciding which actions would make up the game.

The Association voted to remove the provisions for running with the ball and hacking. Blackheath withdrew. Its football did not cease to exist simply because one association would not permit it.

## Agreement was not the same as adoption

Even clubs sympathetic to common laws did not necessarily embrace the first set the FA published.

Lincoln's existing laws had drawn on Marlborough, Eton and Rugby. The club experimented with the association game, then withdrew, saying it had not found those laws satisfactory. Nearby Louth adopted the FA code in February 1864—but with explicit alterations.

These were not two sides of a modern dispute between football and rugby, already understood as sharply separate sports. They were clubs making choices among arrangements that had all recently been called football.

Sheffield provided an especially revealing response. In September 1865 its members resolved to use an offside rule but to retain their old laws in matches against local clubs that did not adopt it. Its surviving match notebook records games played to the newer offside laws and others played to the old rules.

The same club could play two materially different games, according to its opponents and the terms agreed for the fixture.

There was nothing inherently inconsistent about that. Players wanted to play football, but not always the same football, and not always for the same reasons.

## What, then, is the game?

We tend to think of football as an established object with an official rulebook: first there is the game, and then people arrange to play it.

For these nineteenth-century footballers, the order was often the other way round. The desire to play already existed. The question was what rules would let *these particular people* play *these particular opponents*.

The rules were not incidental. They determined the actions the players were permitted to perform and what counted as success. A change to handling, offside or permissible physical contact could fundamentally alter the contest.

But a set of written laws did not acquire authority over all football simply because somebody published it. A code became useful when other people were willing to play under it. If enough clubs adopted it, unfamiliar opponents could meet without inventing an agreement each time. If they rejected it, football continued elsewhere under other rules.

That leaves a distinction often lost when we look backwards from the modern game.

**People could agree that they wanted common rules while disagreeing about what football should be.**

The FA did not settle that by discovering the one objectively correct game. Its members chose among the versions people already knew. Other footballers could accept that choice, negotiate changes, use it only when convenient, or continue with a different game.

What mattered was not just what the laws said. It was whether the people who wanted a game were willing to play by them.

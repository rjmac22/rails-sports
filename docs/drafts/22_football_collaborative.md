## Agreed opening

Football was invented in 1863.

Before then, people across Britain played all sorts of games they called football. Different schools and clubs had different rules, and nobody could agree on how the game should be played.

Then the Football Association came along, established a common set of rules, and the beautiful game was born.

Or so the story goes.

People had been playing games called football for centuries. Medieval versions varied from place to place, but they weren't entirely without rules. Players followed local customs, even if those rules weren't written down.

By the nineteenth century, Britain's public schools had developed their own versions of football. At Rugby, boys could run with the ball. At Eton, they played games that relied much more on kicking, while Harrow and Winchester had their own rules and traditions. These weren't incomplete versions of a game waiting to be invented. They were established games that worked perfectly well for the boys playing them.


Leaving school didn't necessarily mean giving up football.

In London, former pupils of Blackheath Proprietary School continued playing the game they'd learned as boys. They enjoyed their particular version of football and wanted to carry on playing it.

At first, the games were organised among former pupils. But when there weren't enough of them, the group began inviting outsiders. What had started as a way for old schoolmates to continue enjoying football developed into Blackheath Football Club.

They weren't trying to invent a new sport or establish rules for everyone else. They wanted to keep playing a game they already knew.

Other men simply wanted somewhere to play football regularly.

Sheffield Football Club was established in 1857. Its members paid subscriptions, bought footballs and arranged games on Saturday afternoons. They even developed their own written rules.

But they didn't need another club to play against. With enough members to make two teams, they could play among themselves. According to one early member's later recollection, they sometimes divided the players alphabetically to decide who played on which side.

They had organised a club, written rules and established regular football matches, all without needing anyone outside Sheffield to agree on how the game should be played.

Football wasn't always the main attraction.

In Melbourne in 1858, cricketer Tom Wills suggested forming a football club to keep players active during the winter. He even thought running around on the cricket ground might help firm up the turf.

But Wills wasn't proposing that they adopt somebody else's game. He wanted the club to establish a committee to write its own rules.

And if football wasn't practical? He suggested a rifle club or other athletic activities instead.

The following year, Wills helped establish the rules of a new version of football. It would eventually become Australian rules.

The objective was to keep cricketers active. Football was one possible way of doing it, and its rules could be designed by the people who wanted to play.


But wanting to play football was one thing. Getting enough people together to play was another.

A football club could make that easier. Members knew when and where to meet, equipment could be shared, and somebody could take responsibility for organising the games.

Barnes Football Club's rules of 1862 even allowed members to invite friends and local residents to play. Any four members could collect the club's footballs from the White Hart pub.

You didn't necessarily need a fixed team or a full committee meeting every time somebody fancied a game.

Some football clubs began at work.

In 1878, employees at the Lancashire and Yorkshire Railway's carriage and wagon works in Newton Heath established a football team. Their superintendent, Frederick Attock, became its first president. The railway helped the club find a ground and paid the rent; the works dining room was used for club business. At first the team played other railway departments and companies.

It's a type of football club that still exists. In Japan, Honda operates its own team, founded in 1971, with players who are also company employees. In 2023, captain Kazuki Matsumoto described assembling parts for marine engines in the morning before football training began at 2.30pm. The team competes in Japan's national Football League.

Newton Heath eventually took a different path. As it recruited professional footballers and spent money improving its ground, its ambitions outgrew the railway's willingness to support them. The club separated from the company in 1892. It would later become Manchester United.

Not everyone who established a football club was primarily interested in playing football.

In Glasgow in 1887, Brother Walfrid helped establish Celtic Football Club to raise money for charitable work in the city's impoverished East End. Football matches could attract spectators, and spectators could provide the money needed to help feed people.

The players might have wanted competition, enjoyment or recognition. But for Walfrid, football offered something else: a way to finance charitable work.

Here was a club established not simply to provide games for its members, but to achieve something beyond the football pitch.

Sometimes a football club was established because someone had a football ground but no team to play on it.

In 1892, Everton left Anfield following a dispute with the ground's owner and the club's former president, John Houlding. The disagreement involved rent and other business arrangements.

Houlding was left with a football ground but no football club. Rather than abandon the venture, he established a new team to play there: Liverpool Football Club.

He wasn't looking for some friends to have a kickabout. Football was already attracting paying spectators, and Houlding had a valuable ground he wanted to put to use.

### Agreed illustration to use (placement/wording still to edit together)

**Pub pool analogy, proposed for the transition to negotiating matches with outsiders:** Friends play pool together down the pub under their shared, half-remembered local rules. Nobody needs to check every rule; they're having fun and aren't good enough to worry about every detail. Then a stranger joins and asks whether a foul means two shots, whether you can play backwards, and so on. Everyone groans, and they might agree to use an official code just to get on with the game. **They have agreed to a common code for convenience, not necessarily because they prefer it.** If one rule matters to them, they might refuse. Keep it vivid/brief, not a lecture. This is a *modern analogy*, not historical evidence of how any particular Victorian club behaved.

### Important motivation/mechanism not yet written as prose — participation coordination

Players can enjoy football but still fail to get a game if not enough turn up. A formal club can provide consistent times and venue, a pool of available members, equipment and rules governing guest participation. **Distinguish why people *want* to play (exercise, pleasure, etc.) from why *organising a club* helps people play regularly.** Don't assert that attendance was the documented *founding motive* of every club: mostly a reason a club structure can help.

Evidence: Blackheath's 1858–62 attempted old-boys-only side couldn't reliably get enough players, admitted outsiders and formalised as an open club (club's later history: https://blackheathrugby.co.uk/our-history/). Barnes 1862 rules explicitly allow members' friends and local residents and store footballs at White Hart; 4 members can borrow them (contemporary club rules transcribed https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Rules_of_Barnes_Football_Club_(1862)). Queen's Park 1867 formalised regular play days (Monday/Wednesday/Saturday), ball/flag custodians, venue and membership (Robinson 1920 account of early bylaws, https://electricscotland.com/hiStory/sport/football/chapter6.htm). This makes the *reliability/turnout* issue concrete. User compared their pub poker group: uncertain participation can cause people to skip, exacerbating the problem. These are explanatory mechanisms, not a claim every club had poor attendance.
